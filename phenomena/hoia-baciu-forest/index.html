Full Account

Hoia-Baciu Forest (Romanian: Pădurea Hoia-Baciu) is a forest of approximately 250–295 hectares (about 3 km²) on the western edge of Cluj-Napoca in Cluj County, Transylvania, Romania, adjacent to the open-air section of the Ethnographic Museum of Transylvania. Since the 1960s it has been promoted as a hotspot of paranormal phenomena and is now widely nicknamed the 'Bermuda Triangle of Transylvania'. According to the most-circulated local legend, the forest takes its name from a shepherd called Baciu (Romanian for 'shepherd' or 'head shepherd') who is said to have vanished in the forest with his flock of about 200 sheep; folklorists note that the legend has no documented date or corroborating record. The forest gained international notoriety after biologist Alexandru Sift began taking photographs of unusual phenomena in the area from the late 1950s, and after military technician Emil Barnea's photographs of a disc-shaped object over the forest on 18 August 1968 became some of the most-circulated UFO images associated with Romania. The forest also contains a circular clearing, the Poiana Rotundă ('round glade'), where vegetation reportedly fails to grow normally, and is famous for unusually twisted, contorted trees. Reported phenomena include ghosts, sudden anxiety and nausea, electronic device malfunctions, unaccountable rashes and burns, and missing-time experiences. Skeptical commentators stress that no confirmed death, disappearance, or instrumentally documented paranormal event is on record.

Etymology

Origin:
Romanian. 'Hoia' is an old local toponym; 'Baciu' is the Romanian word for a head shepherd. The forest's name is traditionally explained by a legend of a shepherd, Baciu, who is said to have disappeared in the woods with his sheep.
Meaning:
'Hoia of the head shepherd', or simply 'Hoia [forest of] Baciu (the shepherd)'.
First Use:
Local Romanian usage; widely adopted in international paranormal literature from the late 20th century onward.
