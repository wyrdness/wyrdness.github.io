Ifrit

entity_spirit Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

An ifrit is a powerful, often malevolent class of jinn in Arabic and Islamic tradition, frequently associated with fire, the underworld, and the spirits of the violently slain.

Details

Origin:
Ifrit belief is distributed across the Arabic-speaking world and the broader Islamic cultural sphere, including the Mashriq, Egypt, North Africa, the Persian world, and parts of South and Southeast Asia.
Regions:
Arabian Peninsula, Mashriq, Maghreb, Egypt, Levant, Persian world
First Reported:
7th c. CE

Full Account

The ifrit (Arabic عفريت, ʿifrīt; pl. عفاريت, ʿafārīt) is a class of supernatural being in Arabic and Islamic tradition, generally classified as a powerful and dangerous jinn. The Qur'an mentions an ifrit in Sūrat an-Naml (27:38–40), where one of the jinn before Solomon offers to bring the throne of Bilqīs (Queen of Sheba) before the king rises. Hadith and tafsīr literature describe ifrits as fire-formed, capable of shapeshifting and great strength, but also constrained: they can be compelled by sorcerers and bound by the seal of Solomon. In Egyptian folk Islam ifrits are linked specifically to the souls of those killed by violence, drawn to the spilled blood of the victim; in Moroccan tradition they are conceived as a more substantial and physically deformed kind of demon, with claw-like hands, flaming eyes, and sometimes seven heads. Ifrits also figure prominently in the One Thousand and One Nights, where they appear in tales such as 'The Fisherman and the Jinni' and 'The Porter and the Three Ladies of Baghdad'. Although usually portrayed as wicked, classical and modern Muslim scholarship is clear that ifrits, like other jinn, are moral agents who are not fundamentally evil and may even serve God's purposes.

Etymology

Origin:
Arabic عفر ('afara), 'to rub with dust' or 'roll into dust', the root of عفريت ('ifrīt). Some Western scholars have proposed Middle Persian āfrītan ('to create') as a possible foreign source, with the Avestan spirit āfriti- offered as a possible ultimate origin (Cheung), but this remains contested.
Meaning:
'Powerful, cunning, malevolent demon'; in Islamic usage almost always followed by the phrase 'among the jinn'.
First Use:
Earliest extant Arabic Islamic attestations are in the Qur'an (7th c. CE, Sūrat an-Naml 27:39) and in early hadith collections.

Cultural Significance

Ifrits inhabit ruins, deserts, and places of violent death; can be compelled by sorcery and bound by the Seal of Solomon; nail driven into the blood of a murder victim prevents the formation of an ifrit (Egyptian folk Islam).

Related Phenomena

  • Jinn — The general class of fire-formed supernatural beings of which ifrits are a powerful subtype
  • Marid — Another powerful class of jinn, often substituted for ifrits in folkloric narratives
  • Shaitan — Devils of Islamic tradition; some classical sources blur the boundary with ifrits
  • Ghūl — Shapeshifting desert demon of Arabic folklore
  • Iblīs — The leader of the shayāṭīn; in some narratives summons ifrits to test prophets
