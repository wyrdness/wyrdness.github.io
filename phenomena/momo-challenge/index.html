Momo Challenge hoax

urban_legend Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

The 'Momo Challenge' was a 2018-2019 internet hoax and media-driven moral panic alleging that a sinister character named 'Momo' contacted children via WhatsApp and YouTube and instructed them to perform self-harm; investigators, platforms and child-safety organisations found no evidence of any such challenge.

Details

Origin:
Global, on the English-language internet, with substantial coverage in Latin America, India, Europe and North America.
Regions:
WhatsApp messaging networks, YouTube and YouTube Kids, Mainstream tabloid news media
First Reported:
2018-07

Full Account

The Momo Challenge was an internet hoax and moral panic that circulated globally in 2018 and peaked in February-March 2019. It alleged that an entity called 'Momo' — visually represented by a Japanese sculpture of a long-haired, bulging-eyed female figure — contacted children via WhatsApp and embedded videos on YouTube, instructing them to perform self-harm or suicide tasks. The image used was 'Mother Bird', a sculpture by Japanese artist Keisuke Aiso (also reported as Keisuke Aiso of Link Factory), originally exhibited in 2016 and not created in connection with any challenge. Police and child-safety organisations including the NSPCC, Samaritans, the UK Safer Internet Centre and India's Central Bureau of Investigation declared the challenge unfounded. YouTube reported finding no videos showing or promoting any such challenge and demonetised related content. The episode is now widely studied as a textbook moral panic generated by social-media chain warnings and amplified by mainstream media coverage.

The name 'Momo' was attached online to the 'Mother Bird' sculpture by Japanese artist Keisuke Aiso (Link Factory), exhibited in 2016.

An invented internet handle for the avatar image; not connected to a real entity or person.
First Use:
2018, in early WhatsApp / YouTube chain warnings.

Timeline

  • 2016-01-01 — Japanese artist Keisuke Aiso (Link Factory) exhibits the sculpture 'Mother Bird' at Tokyo's vanilla gallery; the sculpture is unrelated to any challenge (Origin of the image later attached to the hoax)
  • 2018-07-01 — Initial YouTube discussions of an alleged self-harm phenomenon similar to the Blue Whale Challenge (Earliest online traces of the Momo Challenge narrative)
  • 2018-08-01 — Police forces in Argentina, Germany, Luxembourg, Spain, Canada, Mexico and the USA issue warnings; news reports allege WhatsApp self-harm messages in South America and India (First international press wave)
  • 2018-08-29 — India's Central Bureau of Investigation declares the claims 'far fetched and devoid of any evidence' and notes 'the game has not claimed any victim' (Major government debunking)
  • 2018-01-01 — Keisuke Aiso destroys the original 'Mother Bird' sculpture (which had been disintegrating naturally) (Symbolic end of the image's physical existence)
  • 2019-02-01 — Global moral-panic peak: Police Service of Northern Ireland and Kim Kardashian post public warnings (Peak of mainstream attention)
  • 2019-03-01 — NSPCC, Samaritans, UK Safer Internet Centre and child-safety experts urge media to stop amplifying the unfounded panic; YouTube reports finding no videos promoting any such challenge and demonetises Momo content (Coordinated debunking)

The Momo Challenge is a documented hoax; belief in it is best understood as a sociological phenomenon rather than a description of any real entity or activity.
