Supay

Overview

Supay is a significant deity in Andean (Inca/Quechua) mythology who rules the underworld realm Ukhu Pacha; originally an ambivalent god of death, he was recast by Spanish colonial missionaries as equivalent to the Christian devil, a syncretic transformation that persists in modern Andean culture.

Full Account

Supay (also spelled Zupay, Çupay, or Supai) is one of the most significant supernatural entities in Andean mythology, serving in the pre-Columbian Inca religious system as the deity presiding over Ukhu Pacha — the inner world or underworld realm beneath the earth. The Inca cosmos was divided into three realms: Hanan Pacha (the upper world), Kay Pacha (the middle world of living humans), and Ukhu Pacha, with Supay as its ruler. Originally, Supay was not inherently evil but was understood as an ambivalent force — a necessary counterpart governing death and mineral wealth. He could protect the dignified dead while tormenting others, embodying balance between opposing forces. This nuanced pre-Columbian understanding was fundamentally altered during the Spanish colonial period (beginning 1532), when Catholic missionaries, particularly Fray Domingo de Santo Tomás and subsequent chroniclers, reinterpreted Supay as equivalent to the Christian devil to facilitate conversion. The Quechua word for Supay — meaning 'shadow,' 'soul,' or 'death' — was repurposed as the translation for 'devil' in early Quechua catechisms, fundamentally demonizing the indigenous deity. Today, Supay is syncretically understood throughout the Andean region as both a feared underworld entity and a powerful protective spirit propitiated for mineral wealth. In Bolivian mining communities, he merges with the figure of El Tío, a deity of the underground who must be appeased with offerings to ensure miner safety. The Diablada dance at the Carnaval de Oruro in Bolivia features Supay as the principal devil figure, a dramatic fusion of pre-Columbian and Catholic traditions.

Etymology

Origin:
Quechua language of the Andean peoples. The name derives from the Quechua root 'supa,' documented by Spanish chroniclers as meaning shadow, illness, death, or soul/anima.
Meaning:
Shadow, soul, illness, death; in colonial usage reinterpreted as 'devil'
First Use:
Documented in early colonial Quechua-Spanish vocabularies, notably by Fray Domingo de Santo Tomás (1560). Pre-Columbian oral use is older but undated.

Cultural Significance

In traditional Andean belief, Supay governs the dead and the mineral riches of the earth. Mining communities maintain active ritual relationships with Supay through El Tío — offering coca leaves, alcohol, cigarettes, and llama blood at the mine entrance. The relationship is pragmatic and respectful rather than purely fearful: Supay is a powerful entity who must be honored, not merely avoided.
