Overview

A class of demons of Iranian and broader Persianate mythology, descended from the Avestan daēvas demonised under Zoroastrianism. Most famous as the divs of Ferdowsi's Shāhnāmeh — gigantic, horned, monstrous beings, including Div-e Sefid (the White Div) and Akvan Div — who oppose the hero Rostam.

Full Account

The div (Persian دیو, dīv; from Avestan daēva, 'false god') is a malevolent supernatural being of Iranian mythology with cognates and reflexes across the Persianate world (Tajik, Pashto, Kurdish, Azerbaijani, Armenian, Georgian, and Turkic traditions). In Zoroastrian theology, the daēvas are demons aligned with Angra Mainyu (Ahriman) against Ahura Mazda; the Avestan Vendīdād (literally 'law against the daēvas') prescribes ritual measures against them. After the Islamic conquest of Iran in the 7th century CE, the older daēva category was reshaped into the dīv of New Persian literature — gigantic, often horned and tusked humanoid creatures, frequently shape-shifters, dwelling in distant mountains and caves. The most famous div narratives are in Ferdowsi's Shāhnāmeh (completed c. 1010 CE): the Haft-Khān (Seven Labours) of Rostam culminate in the slaying of Div-e Sefid, the white div of Mazandaran, whose blood cures the blinded Kay Kāvus; and the Akvan Div episode, in which the demon hurls a sleeping Rostam into the sea. In folk tradition the divs play roles closer to ogres and giants of European folklore — to be tricked, blinded, and overcome by clever heroes — while in Sufi allegory they are read as personifications of the lower self (nafs) and of moral vice.

Etymology

Origin:
Indo-Iranian *daiwa- ('heavenly being'), cognate with Sanskrit deva ('god') and Latin deus. In Zoroastrian usage the term was inverted to denote false gods and demons.
Meaning:
Demon, ogre, or evil spirit; in Zoroastrianism, a malign being aligned with Angra Mainyu.
First Use:
Avestan texts of the early-to-mid 1st millennium BCE; New Persian dīv attested from the 9th century CE onward, with full literary development in Ferdowsi's Shāhnāmeh (c. 977–1010 CE).

Cultural Significance

In contemporary Iranian and Caucasian folk traditions divs function as ogres of folktale, while in religious-historical context they are part of Zoroastrian demonology.
