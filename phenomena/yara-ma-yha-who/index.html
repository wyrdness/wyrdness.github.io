Yara-ma-yha-who

Overview

The Yara-ma-yha-who is a small, red, vampire-like humanoid of southeastern Australian Aboriginal folklore, depicted with suckers on its hands and feet that drain a victim's blood; it serves as a cautionary figure warning against wandering alone beneath wild fig trees.

Details

Origin:
The legend is recorded from Aboriginal traditions of southeastern Australia. Published English-language compilations do not consistently localise it to a single nation; the story circulates in the broader cultural region documented in early-20th-century ethnographies.
Regions:
Southeastern Australia, New South Wales, Victoria
First Reported:

Full Account

The Yara-ma-yha-who is a creature from the folklore of Aboriginal peoples of southeastern Australia, recorded in early-20th-century English-language ethnographic compilations. It is described as a small, red, frog-like humanoid roughly four feet tall, with a disproportionately large head, a wide toothless mouth, and octopus-like suckers on the tips of its fingers and toes. According to the legend, the creature inhabits wild fig trees and ambushes travellers — particularly children — who rest beneath them. It descends, attaches itself to the victim, and drains blood through its suckers. Once the victim is too weak to resist, the Yara-ma-yha-who swallows them whole, drinks water, and naps; it later regurgitates the victim alive but slightly shorter and with reddened skin. Repeated capture causes a person to shrink and gradually transform into a Yara-ma-yha-who themselves. The creature is said to be active during the day and to target only living prey; the recommended counter-strategy in the stories is to feign death until sunset. The story functions as a teaching narrative — warning children against wandering alone, encoding caution about specific landscape features, and (in some readings) as a spiritual enforcer of country rather than a 'demon' in the European sense.

Etymology

Origin:
Name recorded in English-language ethnographic sources of the early 20th century; the original Aboriginal language of attribution is not consistently identified across published sources.
Meaning:
Glossed in English-language compilations as the name of the creature itself; no widely accepted literal translation appears in the secondary literature available.
First Use:
Earliest scholarly English documentation in W. Ramsay Smith's 'Myths and Legends of the Australian Aboriginals' (1930).

Timeline

  • Pre-contact — Yara-ma-yha-who story circulates in southeastern Aboriginal oral tradition as a cautionary narrative (Cultural baseline pre-dating colonial documentation)
  • 1930 — W. Ramsay Smith publishes 'Myths and Legends of the Australian Aboriginals', incorporating manuscripts by David Unaipon and including the Yara-ma-yha-who story (Earliest widely-cited English-language documentation of the legend)
  • 2001 — Melbourne University Press publishes 'Legendary Tales of the Australian Aborigines' restoring David Unaipon's authorship of the manuscripts that Smith had drawn on (Re-attribution of the Yara-ma-yha-who narrative to its Aboriginal author)

Cultural Significance

The Yara-ma-yha-who is described in published Aboriginal narrative collections as a being one should not seek out; the right response is avoidance, and (within the story) feigning death until sunset. The narrative encodes practical caution about lone travel and specific tree species.
