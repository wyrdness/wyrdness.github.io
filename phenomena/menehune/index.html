Menehune

fae_folklore Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

A legendary race of small, industrious people in Hawaiian tradition, said to have inhabited the islands before Polynesian settlers and to have built stonework, fishponds, and heiau by night — most famously the Menehune (Alekoko) Fishpond and the Kīkīaola ditch on Kauaʻi.

Details

Origin:
The Menehune are a tradition of the Hawaiian Islands, with the densest cycle of stories on the island of Kauaʻi and adjacent Oʻahu.
Regions:
Hawaiian archipelago, Kauaʻi, Oʻahu, Hawaiʻi (Big Island)
First Reported:
Pre-contact

Full Account

The Menehune are a legendary people of Hawaiian oral tradition, generally described as small in stature (often given as about two feet tall), powerfully built, and master craftspeople — particularly in stonework, fishpond construction, ditch engineering, canoe-building, and heiau (temple) construction. According to Hawaiian tradition the Menehune work only at night, must complete a project in a single night, and abandon it forever if interrupted or if dawn breaks first. Major archaeological structures attributed to them include the Menehune (Alekoko) Fishpond at Niumalu on Kauaʻi, the Kīkīaola ditch (an ancient irrigation aqueduct) at Waimea on Kauaʻi, structures on Necker Island (Mokumanamana), the Pa o ka Menehune breakwater at Kahaluʻu Bay on Hawaiʻi, and the Ulupō Heiau at Kailua, Oʻahu. Folklorist Katharine Luomala argued that the Menehune tradition is a post-European-contact development of the Hawaiian word manahune ('lowly' or 'commoner'), possibly conflated with European brownie folklore. Census records from 1820 list 65 people on Kauaʻi as 'menehune', and an earlier traditional census from the Wainiha Valley reportedly counted 65.

Also Known As

  • Manahune (Hawaiian / Tahitian)

Etymology

Origin:
Hawaiian; cognate with Tahitian manahune. Folklorist Katharine Luomala (1951) argued that the modern fairy-tale 'Menehune' developed by adaptation of the Hawaiian word manahune.
Meaning:
Cognate Tahitian manahune means 'lowly people' or 'commoner' (i.e. of low social status), a sense Luomala argued underlies the Hawaiian usage.
First Use:
Pre-contact Hawaiian oral tradition; first systematically discussed in print by Abraham Fornander in the late 19th century

Timeline

  • 1820-01-01 — Census of Kauaʻi lists 65 individuals as 'menehune' (Suggestion of an actual sub-population identified by the term)
