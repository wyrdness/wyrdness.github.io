Mara

Overview

A female nightmare spirit of Germanic and Slavic folklore who 'rides' sleepers and presses on their chests, producing the experience that gave English the word 'nightmare' (literally 'night-mara').

Full Account

The Mara is a malevolent female spirit of Germanic, Scandinavian, and Slavic folklore who visits sleepers at night, sits or 'rides' on their chests, and produces suffocating dreams, sleep paralysis, and night-sweats. Old English mære, Old Norse mara, and Old High German mara all derive from a Proto-Germanic *marōn, ultimately from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning 'to crush, press, oppress'. Modern English 'nightmare' is literally 'night-Mara'; French cauchemar borrows the same element via Old French. In the 13th-century Ynglinga saga (preserved in Snorri Sturluson's Heimskringla), King Vanlandi Sveigðisson of Uppsala is killed by a mara conjured by the Sámi sorceress Huld — the saga describes how the creature first 'trod on his legs', then crushed his head — the earliest recorded mara incident in Scandinavia. In Slavic tradition (Polish zmora, Czech můra, South Slavic mora), the Mara may be either a demonic being or the wandering soul of a living person, often a woman, with traits such as a connected unibrow used to identify her; protective practices included placing brooms or upside-down slippers by the bed and offering food.

Etymology

Origin:
Proto-Germanic *marōn (compare Old English mære, Old Norse mara, Old High German mara), from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning 'to crush' or 'to press'. The same root underlies Slavic mora / mara.
Meaning:
'Crusher' or 'oppressor' — referring to the suffocating chest-pressure experienced by victims.
First Use:
Attested in Old English and Old Norse from the early medieval period; the compound 'nightmare' (Middle English nyght-mare) is attested by the late 13th–14th century.

Cultural Significance

Across Germanic and Slavic Europe, the Mara was treated as a real cause of bad dreams and chest-pressure during sleep, and protective practices were widespread.
