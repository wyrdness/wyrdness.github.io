La Llorona

ghost_haunting Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

A weeping female ghost said to haunt rivers, lakes, and irrigation channels across Mexico, Central America, parts of South America, and the southwestern United States, mourning the children she drowned. Among the most widespread and durable legends in the Spanish-speaking Americas.

Details

Origin:
La Llorona is widespread across the Spanish-speaking Americas. Core regions are Mexico (especially central Mexico and the Mexico City basin), the U.S.-Mexico border, and the southwestern United States, with strong variants throughout Central America, in Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, and as far south as Uruguay. Significant regional names include María Pardo and Tarumama in Colombia and La Sayona in Venezuela.
Regions:
Central Mexico, U.S.-Mexico border, Southwestern United States, Central America, Andean South America, Río de la Plata
First Reported:
c. 1550–1585

Full Account

La Llorona ('the weeping woman') is the central female revenant of Mexican and Mexican-American folklore. The standard narrative tells of a woman — most often called María — who, betrayed or abandoned by her husband or lover, drowns her children in a river in a fit of jealousy or madness, then either drowns herself or is condemned to wander the waterways of the Earth weeping for them. Her cry of '¡Ay, mis hijos!' ('Oh, my children!') is heard near rivers, lakes, irrigation ditches (acequias), and arroyos at night. Sightings describe a tall woman in white with long dark hair, sometimes with a horse-like or skull-like face. Folklorists trace La Llorona's pre-Columbian roots to the Aztec mother-goddess Cihuacóatl, identified in Bernardino de Sahagún's mid-16th-century Florentine Codex as the source of a weeping omen heard before the Spanish conquest, and to Chocacíhuatl ('weeping woman'), one of the cihuateteo — spirits of women who died in childbirth. From the colonial period onward, the legend was used to discipline women and children and to warn against dangerous waterways; from the 1980s, Chicana scholars including Domino Renee Pérez and Gloria Anzaldúa have re-read La Llorona as a figure of resistance, mourning under colonialism, and Chicana identity. Variants exist across Mexico, Guatemala, Honduras, El Salvador, Costa Rica, Panama, Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, Chile, Uruguay, and the southwestern United States.

Etymology

Origin:
Spanish 'la llorona', from the verb llorar ('to cry, to weep') and the agentive suffix -ona, giving 'the (great) weeping woman'.
Meaning:
The weeping woman; the crying woman.
First Use:
Mid-16th century in colonial Mexican Spanish; Bernardino de Sahagún records a 'woman heard going along weeping and shouting' as the sixth omen of the Spanish conquest in the Florentine Codex (Book 12).

Timeline

  • Pre-1519 — Cihuacóatl, mother-goddess of the Aztec Triple Alliance, has an established role as a weeping omen-figure (Pre-Columbian root cited by Sahagún)
  • c. 1550–1585 — Bernardino de Sahagún records the weeping omen of the conquest in the Florentine Codex (Earliest written attestation that scholars connect to La Llorona)
  • 17th–18th century — La Llorona narrative coalesces in colonial New Spain, often in the form of a betrayed Indigenous or mestiza woman drowning her children (Colonial-period consolidation)
  • 1933 — La Llorona, directed by Ramón Peón, becomes the first Mexican sound horror film and the first to put La Llorona on screen (Mass-media debut of the legend)
  • 1986 — Gloria Anzaldúa publishes Borderlands/La Frontera: The New Mestiza, treating La Llorona as a key figure of Chicana identity (Foundational Chicana feminist re-reading)
  • 1991 — Sandra Cisneros publishes the short story 'Woman Hollering Creek' (Major literary reworking of the Llorona figure)
  • 2008 — Domino Renee Pérez publishes There Was a Woman: La Llorona from Folklore to Popular Culture (Most cited modern academic synthesis of the legend)
  • 2017-11-22 — Disney/Pixar's Coco features the folk song 'La Llorona' (made famous by Chavela Vargas) (Mass global re-introduction of the song associated with the legend)
  • 2019-04-19 — Warner Bros. releases The Curse of La Llorona, directed by Michael Chaves (Major Hollywood film tied to The Conjuring franchise)

Cultural Significance

La Llorona is widely treated as a real spirit by traditional storytellers, while simultaneously functioning as a moral and pedagogical figure for children and as a literary symbol of Chicana resistance and mourning.
