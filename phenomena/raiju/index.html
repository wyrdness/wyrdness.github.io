Overview

Raiju (雷獣, 'thunder beast') is a legendary yōkai from Japanese mythology, the companion of Raijin the thunder god, whose body is composed of or wrapped in lightning. It descends during thunderstorms, becomes agitated, and may take many animal forms — most often a weasel, wolf, or cat — depending on the regional tradition.

Details

Origin:
Raiju legends are primarily concentrated in East Japan, with notable regional variants throughout the Japanese archipelago. Most documented accounts come from the Edo period, with locations including Akita, Shinano, Echigo, Sagami, Aizu, Lake Biwa, and Akō.
Regions:
Kantō, Tōhoku, Chūbu, Kinki, East Japan

Full Account

Raiju (雷獣) is a legendary creature and yōkai in Japanese mythology, whose name translates as 'thunder beast' or 'thunder animal.' It is the companion of Raijin, the Shinto god of lightning, and its body is described as composed of or wrapped in lightning. During thunderstorms, the raiju descends from the heavens where it normally resides with Raijin, becoming agitated and leaping about in trees and buildings. Lightning-struck trees were said to bear claw marks left by the raiju. The creature's physical form is notably variable across time and region: most commonly it is depicted as a weasel-like creature, a white-blue wolf or dog with a split tail, a badger-like tanuki, a rabbit, a porcupine, a cat with bat-like flying membranes, or even a pure ball of lightning with no animal form. Western Japanese traditions describe dramatically different appearances, including crab or spider-like creatures with scaled pincer-tipped limbs. According to Edo-period belief, the raiju sleeps in human navels during calm weather — a belief that generated widespread anxiety, since Raijin, searching for his sleeping companion during storms, might strike the sleeping place with lightning, inadvertently killing the human host. This gave rise to protective customs against sleeping outdoors during storms. Several historical 'raiju relics' exist: Saishō-ji Temple in Niigata houses a mummy approximately 35 cm long identified by yōkai researcher Katsumi Tada as a domestic cat, and Yūzan-ji Temple in Iwate contains a mummy tagged 'Raijin.' Modern scholars identify the masked palm civet as the most likely basis for the raiju, given the animal's size, tree-climbing behavior, sharp claws, and tree-scratching habits. Many Edo-period raiju specimens displayed in sideshows were fabricated from weasels or badgers.

Etymology

Origin:
Japanese; compound of 雷 (rai, 'thunder/lightning') and 獣 (jū, 'beast/animal'). Legends are primarily concentrated in East Japan and were especially prominent during the Edo period (1603–1868).
Meaning:
Thunder beast / thunder animal
First Use:
Edo period (1603–1868) documentation; roots in older Shinto traditions associating animals with lightning phenomena

Cultural Significance

During the Edo period the raiju was 'extremely well known' and reflected genuine anxiety about lightning. Protective practices (sleeping face-down, carrying specific protective items) were widespread.
