Eloko

entity_spirit Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

A dangerous dwarf-like forest spirit of the Mongo-Nkundo peoples of the Congo Basin, said to dwell in hollow trees, dressed in leaves with a beard of grass, carrying enchanting bells and possessing claws and a wide-opening mouth. Often understood as the spirit of an ancestor who died in the forest under unjust circumstances.

Details

Origin:
Confined to the rainforests of the central Congo Basin, particularly the territory of the Mongo-Nkundo peoples of the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

Full Account

Eloko (plural Biloko) is a class of small, malevolent forest spirit in the folklore of the Mongo-Nkundo peoples of central Democratic Republic of the Congo. According to Jan Knappert's 'African Mythology: An Encyclopedia of Myth and Legend' (1995) and standard summaries on Wikipedia, Biloko inhabit hollow trees deep in the rainforest, are clothed in leaves rather than fur, have grass for a beard, and carry small bells whose sound bewitches passers-by. They have piercing eyes, long sharp claws, and snouts capable of opening wide enough to swallow a human. They are said to be the spirits of ancestors who perished in the forest — through accident, murder, or improper burial — and to guard the rare game and fruits of the deep wood. Biloko jealously defend their territories: only the most courageous hunters can enter their domains, and only with strong protective magic, an amulet or a fetish. Biloko prey particularly on women, whom they lure with bell-songs in childlike voices. In Mongo-Nkundo cosmology the figure functions both as a category of dangerous spirit and as a powerful taboo against straying alone into the deep forest.

Etymology

Origin:
Mongo-Nkundo (a Bantu language of the Congo Basin); the noun class prefix bi- forms the plural 'biloko' from the singular 'eloko'.
Meaning:
In Mongo-Nkundo usage, eloko refers to a small forest spirit or 'thing of the forest'.
First Use:
Long pre-colonial oral tradition; documented in 20th-century African ethnographic and folkloric literature (notably Knappert, 1995).

Cultural Significance

Within Mongo-Nkundo tradition, the deep forest is understood as Biloko-territory and is to be entered only with proper ritual precaution.
