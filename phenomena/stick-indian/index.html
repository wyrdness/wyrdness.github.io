Stick Indian

entity_spirit Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

A category of forest beings in the traditions of many Pacific Northwest Indigenous peoples (Salish, Yakama, Klickitat, Cayuse, Nez Perce, and others). Variously described as tall hairy giants or as small forest dwarves, they are most commonly known for whistling at travellers, leading people astray, and punishing those who disrespect the forest.

Details

Origin:
Traditional homelands of Pacific Northwest peoples whose oral traditions include Stick Indian / tsiatko / seeahtik beings — including the Coast Salish region of present-day Washington and southwestern British Columbia, the Plateau (Yakama, Klickitat, Cayuse, Nez Perce, Wenatchi, Colville, Spokane), and the Olympic Peninsula.
Regions:
Pacific Northwest, Puget Sound, Olympic Peninsula, Columbia Plateau, Yakama lands, Coast Salish territories
First Reported:
Pre-contact

Full Account

'Stick Indian' is an English-language cover term for a class of dangerous forest beings appearing in the oral traditions of many Pacific Northwest Indigenous peoples — including the Salish-speaking Coast Salish and Puget Sound peoples, the Yakama, Klickitat, Cayuse, Nez Perce, Wenatchi, Colville, and others. The original Indigenous names (e.g. tsiatko, steta'l, seeahtik) refer to entities that vary substantially across nations: Salish traditions often describe them as tall, hairy, bigfoot-like giants who live in the deep forest, whereas Cayuse and Yakama traditions describe them more often as forest dwarves or 'little people'. Across traditions, the Stick Indians are characterised by whistling, mimicry of human voices, and the ability to lead travellers astray, paralyse, hypnotise, or in some accounts kidnap and even devour them. They are commonly invoked in moral instruction — never whistle at night in the woods, never answer a voice that calls your name from the trees, never speak ill of the forest. Many Pacific Northwest people regard 'Stick Indian' as an English euphemism, holding that pronouncing the original Indigenous name aloud risks attracting their attention; this aspect of the tradition remains active today.

Etymology

Origin:
Pacific Northwest Indigenous oral traditions; the English-language phrase 'Stick Indian' is a calque on a Plateau-region usage in which 'stick' translates a word meaning 'forest' or 'woods', so 'Stick Indians' literally means 'Indians of the woods'.
Meaning:
'Stick' in regional Chinook Jargon and Plateau English means 'wood' or 'forest'; the term names beings of the woods. Indigenous names (tsiatko, seeahtik, steta'l) carry their own etymologies in the relevant languages.
First Use:
19th-century Plateau English usage; recorded in early-20th-century ethnographies of Pacific Northwest peoples

Cultural Significance

Stick Indians are widely understood as real persons of the forest who must be respected. Common rules: do not whistle at night; do not answer a voice that calls your name from the trees; do not speak arrogantly or mockingly while hunting; treat the forest with reverence.

Related Phenomena

  • Ts'emekwes — Lummi term for forest hairy giants
