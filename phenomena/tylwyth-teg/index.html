Tylwyth Teg

fae_folklore Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

The Tylwyth Teg ('Fair Family') are the principal fairy folk of Welsh folklore, parallel to the Aos Sí of Ireland and the fairies of English and Continental tradition.

Details

Origin:
Tylwyth Teg traditions are found across Wales, with regional variations in name and emphasis. They are part of the broader Brythonic Celtic fairy tradition shared with Cornwall and Brittany.
Regions:
Wales, Snowdonia / Eryri, South Wales, Glamorgan, Carmarthenshire, Pembrokeshire, Welsh Marches
First Reported:
12th century

Full Account

Y Tylwyth Teg are the most widely known fairies of Welsh folklore, encompassing a varied family of supernatural beings recorded in Welsh oral and literary tradition since at least the 12th century. The category includes household, lake, mountain, and mine fairies, and overlaps with Bendith y Mamau ('Blessing of the Mothers'), Gwyllion, and Ellyllon. They are typically described as fair-haired and beautiful, dwelling in fairy hills, lakes (notably Llyn y Fan Fach), or under green mounds, and entering human society to dance in fairy rings, leave gifts at houses where milk has been set out for them, or — more darkly — to abduct human children, leaving changelings in their place. Iron, salt, and the Christian cross are traditional protections. The term tylwyth teg is first attested in a 14th-century poem attributed to Dafydd ap Gwilym, and references to Welsh fairy folk go back at least to Giraldus Cambrensis (c. 1146–1223). The 19th-century folklorist Wirt Sikes systematized later accounts in his British Goblins (1880), and 20th-century work by John Rhŷs (Celtic Folklore, 1901) remains a standard reference.

Etymology

Origin:
Middle Welsh phrase tylwyth teg, literally 'fair tribe' or 'fair family'. 'Tylwyth' means tribe, family, household; 'teg' means fair, beautiful.
Meaning:
'The Fair Family' or 'the Fair Folk' — euphemistic; like the Irish term Aos Sí, the name avoids invoking the fairies directly.
First Use:
First attested in a 14th-century Welsh poem attributed to Dafydd ap Gwilym; possibly earlier in oral tradition.

Timeline

  • 1191 — Giraldus Cambrensis records the tale of Elidyr's visit to the fairy realm in Itinerarium Cambriae (Among the earliest written Welsh fairy narratives)
  • 14th century — Term 'tylwyth teg' first attested in a poem attributed to Dafydd ap Gwilym (First attestation of the Welsh name)
  • 1880 — Wirt Sikes publishes British Goblins, systematizing Welsh fairy lore including the Tylwyth Teg's five-fold classification (Standard 19th-century synthesis)
  • 1901 — Sir John Rhŷs publishes Celtic Folklore: Welsh and Manx, a major scholarly study of Tylwyth Teg traditions (Foundational academic treatment)

Cultural Significance

Belief that the Tylwyth Teg are powerful, easily offended, fond of cleanliness and reciprocity, and best referred to with respectful or euphemistic names.

Related Phenomena
