Time Slips

anomaly Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

An anomalous experience in which a percipient apparently perceives, or briefly enters, a different time period (most often the past) before returning to the present without the passage of normal time.

Details

Origin:
Reported worldwide; the largest published clusters are in the United Kingdom (Versailles in France, Liverpool, Wiltshire, Norfolk) and the United States.
Regions:
England, Île-de-France, American Southwest, Northeastern United States
First Reported:
1901-08-10

Full Account

Time slips are reports - usually first-person, sometimes shared by multiple witnesses - of having briefly experienced an environment from a different historical period. Witnesses describe period-appropriate clothing, vehicles, signage, architecture, and people, often accompanied by an oppressive 'flat' or 'stage-set' atmospheric quality. The phenomenon was given its first systematically published case in An Adventure (1911) by Charlotte Anne Moberly and Eleanor Jourdain, recounting their 10 August 1901 visit to the Petit Trianon at Versailles. In the United Kingdom the most-discussed cluster is on or near Bold Street, Liverpool, with a much-circulated account from a retired police officer, Frank Williams, in 1996. Within psychical research the experience is typically classified as a form of retrocognition rather than literal time travel; sceptics treat reports as misperception, false memory, or confabulation. The Society for Psychical Research's Psi Encyclopedia maintains an entry on the Versailles case as a benchmark example.

Also Known As

  • Retrocognition (English)
  • Temporal Anomaly (English)

Etymology

Origin:
English compound 'time slip', popularized by 20th-century authors including Joan Forman (The Mask of Time, 1978) and Andrew MacKenzie (Adventures in Time, 1997).
Meaning:
A 'slip' in time during which a witness apparently experiences another era
First Use:
Term entered popular use in the mid-20th century; 'An Adventure' (1911) used the description 'an adventure' rather than 'time slip'.

Timeline

  • 1901-08-10 — Charlotte Anne Moberly and Eleanor Jourdain visit the Petit Trianon at Versailles and report seeing the gardens as in the late 18th century, including figures they later interpret as Marie Antoinette and others (Founding case of the modern time-slip canon)
  • 1911 — Publication of An Adventure under pseudonyms 'Elizabeth Morison' and 'Frances Lamont' (London: Macmillan) (First mainstream time-slip publication; sells out quickly with multiple printings)
  • 1931 — Authors of An Adventure are publicly identified as Charlotte Anne Moberly and Eleanor Jourdain (End of the anonymous period; renewed scholarly attention)
  • 1978 — Joan Forman publishes The Mask of Time (Popular collection of British time-slip cases)
  • 1996-07 — Bold Street, Liverpool: retired police officer Frank Williams reports a brief time-slip in or near the building formerly housing the Cripps store, recounted later on BBC Radio Merseyside's Billy Butler programme (Most-discussed UK time-slip case of the late 20th century; corroborated only by callers who confirmed the historical existence of Cripps)
  • 1997 — Andrew MacKenzie publishes Adventures in Time: Encounters with the Past (Major SPR-affiliated case collection)
