Overview

Mount Shasta is a 14,179-foot (4,322 m) stratovolcano in the southern Cascade Range of Siskiyou County, California, sacred to multiple Indigenous nations (Shasta, Karuk, Modoc, Wintu, Klamath) and a focal point of modern paranormal traditions including the legend of the hidden Lemurian city of Telos and Guy Ballard's 'I AM' Activity.

Full Account

Mount Shasta is a major stratovolcano of the southern Cascade Range, rising 14,179 ft (4,322 m) in Siskiyou County, California (approximately 41.4092 N, 122.1949 W). At about 85 cubic miles, it is the most voluminous stratovolcano in the Cascade Volcanic Arc; it is roughly 593,000 years old and last erupted around 1250. The mountain is sacred to multiple Indigenous nations, including the Shasta (Waka-nunee-Tuki-wuki), Karuk (Úytaahkoo, 'White Mountain'), Modoc, Wintu, and Klamath; in Klamath cosmology Mount Shasta is the home of Skell, Spirit of the Above-World, who battled Llao at Mount Mazama. From the late 19th century onward, Mount Shasta accreted a parallel non-Indigenous esoteric tradition: Frederick Spencer Oliver's 'A Dweller on Two Planets' (written by 1899, published 1905), allegedly channelled from the Lemurian spirit Phylos, was the first published work to link Mount Shasta to a hidden tunnel city of Lemurians, the concept of 'I AM' and the channelling of disembodied spirits. Harvey Spencer Lewis, writing as 'Wishar S. Cerve', published 'Lemuria: The Lost Continent of the Pacific' through the AMORC Rosicrucian order in 1931, cementing the legend in popular imagination. Guy Ballard's encounters with the 'Ascended Master' Saint Germain on the slopes of Mount Shasta in the 1930s founded the I AM Activity, which J. Gordon Melton has identified as the first 'UFO religion'. The mountain has subsequently become a focal point for UFO sighting reports, New Age 'spiritual vortex' practice, claimed Telos / Lemurian encounters, and recurring Bigfoot reports.

Etymology

Origin:
English 'Shasta' is from the Shasta people, indigenous to the surrounding region.
Meaning:
Karuk Úytaahkoo means 'White Mountain'; the Shasta name is Waka-nunee-Tuki-wuki.
First Use:
Indigenous names predate European contact; English 'Mount Shasta' enters the written record in the early 19th century with European-American exploration of northern California.
