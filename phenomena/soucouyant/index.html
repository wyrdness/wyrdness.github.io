Soucouyant

undead Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

The soucouyant is a figure of Caribbean folklore: an old woman by day who at night sheds her skin, transforms into a ball of fire, and flies in search of victims whose blood she drains. She is defeated by finding her shed skin and salting it so she cannot return to human form before sunrise.

Across the Caribbean, including Anglophone, Francophone, and Dutch-speaking territories, with diaspora reach into the United States (notably Louisiana) and Canada.

Full Account

The soucouyant — also spelled soucriant, soukouyan, or soukougnan, and known regionally as the Ole Higue (Guyana, Jamaica), Asema (Suriname), Hag (Bahamas), and Loogaroo (from the French loup-garou) — is a central figure of Anglophone, Francophone, and Dutch Caribbean folklore. By day she appears as a reclusive old woman, often living at the edges of a community. At night she sheds her skin, hides it in a mortar or other vessel, and transforms into a ball of fire that flies through the air seeking sleeping victims. She enters homes through any opening — keyhole, window, crack — and feeds on the blood of her victims, leaving telltale bruises. Excessive feeding can kill the victim or transform them into another soucouyant. Traditional defences include scattering rice or sand around the house (forcing her to count or pick up the grains until dawn), and finding and salting her shed skin so that she cannot put it back on, after which she will burn or die at sunrise. The figure draws on a complex mixture of West African vampiric / fire-being traditions (including parallels with Yoruba aje and the Ashanti obayifo), French and European witch and werewolf traditions (loup-garou), and Indigenous Caribbean and Creole religious frameworks. The soucouyant has been a major figure in Caribbean literature, including in the work of Dionne Brand (Trinidad-Canadian) whose 2007 novel is titled 'Soucouyant'.

Etymology

Origin:
French Caribbean Creole; the etymology of 'soucouyant / soucriant' is contested but is widely traced to French and West African (Fula, Soninke) roots.
Meaning:
Glossed loosely as 'blood-sucker' or 'witch'.
First Use:
Documented in 19th-century West Indian folklore literature and in colonial-era newspaper accounts.

Cultural Significance

Strong continuing folk-belief tradition across Caribbean communities and their diasporas; often invoked in protective practices and storytelling rather than as a casual entertainment figure.
