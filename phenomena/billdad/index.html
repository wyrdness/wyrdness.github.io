Billdad

cryptid Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

A small, kangaroo-legged, hawk-billed amphibious creature of North American lumberjack folklore, said to live only at Boundary Pond in northwestern Maine and to fish by leaping over surface fish and stunning them with a tail-slap.

Details

Origin:
Localised exclusively to Boundary Pond in northwestern Maine, on the Canadian border, per Cox (1910).
Regions:
Maine, Northern Maine lumberwoods, Boundary Pond
First Reported:
1910

Full Account

The Billdad (mock-Latin name Saltipiscator falcorostratus) is a fearsome critter from the tall-tale tradition of North American lumber camps. The creature is described and illustrated by William T. Cox in Fearsome Creatures of the Lumberwoods (1910), the canonical source for the genre. According to Cox, the Billdad lives only around Boundary Pond in northwestern Maine. About the size of a beaver, it has long kangaroo-like hind legs, short forelegs, webbed feet, and a heavy hawk-like bill. It hunts by waiting until a fish breaks the surface to seize an insect, then leaping — reportedly up to 60 yards (about 55 metres) — to land beside it and stun it with a tail-slap before swallowing the prey. Cox's text closes with the comic anecdote of Bill Murphy, a tote-road swamper from Ambegegis, who tasted Billdad stew at a Great Northern Paper Company camp on Hurricane Lake and immediately leapt fifty yards into the lake in imitation of the creature, sinking like a stone. As with the rest of the fearsome critters, the Billdad is a piece of literary folklore — an entertainment, not a serious cryptozoological claim — that lumber-camp storytellers used to play tricks on greenhorns.

Also Known As

  • Saltipiscator falcorostratus (Mock Latin)

Etymology

Origin:
Coined by William T. Cox in Fearsome Creatures of the Lumberwoods (1910), the foundational literary catalogue of North American lumberjack tall-tale fauna.
Meaning:
The 'bill' refers to the hawk-like beak; 'dad' is dialectal for 'fellow'. Cox's mock-binomial Saltipiscator falcorostratus glosses 'leaping fisher with a falcon's beak'.
First Use:
1910 (William T. Cox)

Timeline

  • 1910 — William T. Cox publishes Fearsome Creatures of the Lumberwoods, including the Billdad with description and illustration (Canonical source for the Billdad)
  • 1939 — Henry H. Tryon's Fearsome Critters compiles further such creatures (Second major literary codification of the genre)

Cultural Significance

Universally understood as humorous folklore rather than literal cryptozoology, both today and at the time of Cox's writing.
