Betobeto-san

entity_spirit Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

An invisible Japanese yōkai known only by its sound: the patter of straight-following geta (wooden sandal) footsteps that walks behind people on lonely night roads. Harmless, and easily dismissed by stepping aside and politely inviting it to pass.

Details

Origin:
Reports localised to rural Japan, particularly Uda-gun in Nara Prefecture and parts of Shizuoka Prefecture; related Bishakutsu reports in Fukui Prefecture.
Regions:
Nara Prefecture (Uda-gun), Shizuoka Prefecture, Fukui Prefecture (related variant)
First Reported:
20th century

Full Account

Betobeto-san (べとべとさん) is a Japanese yōkai of the auditory kind — known not by appearance but by the sound of footsteps following a traveller on a quiet night road. The name imitates the 'beto-beto' sound of sandals slapping the ground. The creature is invisible, never harms anyone, and is reported chiefly from Uda-gun in Nara Prefecture and from Shizuoka Prefecture, with related auditory yōkai (Bishakutsu) recorded in Fukui Prefecture. The folkloric remedy is to stop, step aside on the road, and politely say something like 'Betobeto-san, please go ahead' (Betobeto-san, osaki ni okoshi); the footsteps then continue on without you. Betobeto-san is one of the best-known minor yōkai in Japanese folklore compendiums and is most familiar to modern audiences through manga artist Shigeru Mizuki, who included it in his GeGeGe no Kitarō works.

Also Known As

  • べとべとさん (Japanese)
  • Bishakutsu (Japanese, Fukui Prefecture)

Etymology

Origin:
Japanese onomatopoeia: 'beto-beto' (べとべと) imitating the sound of footsteps, with the honorific suffix -san. The yōkai is identified by sound rather than form.
Meaning:
Roughly 'Mr. Sticky-Steps' or 'Mr. Patter-Patter' — named for its distinctive footstep sound.
First Use:
Recorded in 20th-century Japanese folklore compendiums, including the Sōgō Nihon Minzoku Goi (Comprehensive Vocabulary of Japanese Folk Customs, 1955) and the Yōkai Jiten (Yōkai Encyclopedia) tradition.

Timeline

  • 1955 — Betobeto-san appears in the Sōgō Nihon Minzoku Goi (Comprehensive Vocabulary of Japanese Folk Customs) (First major folkloristic reference work to record the yōkai)
  • 1968 — Shigeru Mizuki includes Betobeto-san in his GeGeGe no Kitarō universe (Brings the yōkai into postwar pop culture)
  • 1995 — Murakami Kenji's Yōkai Jiten (Yōkai Encyclopedia) catalogues Betobeto-san with regional notes (Modern reference compilation)

Cultural Significance

Recognised as harmless; the practical 'remedy' (stepping aside and inviting it to pass) is itself part of the tradition and reflects the genteel character of much Japanese yōkai folklore.
