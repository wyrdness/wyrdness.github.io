Dragon

Overview

A class of large, serpentine or reptilian mythological creatures attested across virtually every culture, ranging from the auspicious water-and-rain dragons of East Asia to the fire-breathing, treasure-hoarding antagonists of European Christian and Norse tradition.

Full Account

Dragons are large serpentine or reptilian creatures attested in the mythology and folklore of nearly every ancient culture. The English word descends from Old French dragon and Latin draco, both ultimately from the Ancient Greek drakōn (δράκων, 'huge serpent'), which is plausibly derived from the verb dérkomai ('I see') and the Indo-European root *derḱ-. East Asian traditions — Chinese long, Japanese tatsu/ryū, Korean yong, Vietnamese rồng — present dragons as predominantly benevolent, controlling rain and water and serving as imperial symbols. Western traditions, from Mesopotamian Tiamat and the mušḫuššu, through Egyptian Apep, the Hebrew Leviathan, the Iranian Aži Dahāka, the Greek Typhon, Hydra, and Ladon, the Norse Fafnir, Jörmungandr, and Níðhöggr, the Germanic Beowulf-dragon, the Slavic Zmey Gorynych, and the Christian-era figures of Saint George and the dragon, more often cast dragons as antagonists embodying chaos, drought, or evil. Theories of origin proposed by scholars include David E. Jones's evolutionary fear-of-snakes argument, Adrienne Mayor's case for fossil discoveries inspiring dragon imagery, and Robert Blust's view of dragons as a convergent pre-scientific account of the natural mechanisms of rainfall and the rainbow. The Mesoamerican Quetzalcoatl extends the global pattern with a feathered-serpent deity associated with wind and knowledge.

Etymology

Origin:
Old French dragon < Latin draco < Ancient Greek drakōn (δράκων), 'huge serpent'
Meaning:
Likely linked to the Greek verb dérkomai ('I see'), suggesting a 'sharp-sighted' creature; Indo-European root *derḱ-
First Use:
Greek drakōn used by Homer (Iliad) and Hesiod; English 'dragon' attested from the early 13th century

Cultural Significance

Dragons function as cosmic adversaries in many Indo-European myths, as imperial and meteorological symbols across East Asia, and as guardians of cultural identity in heraldry and national symbolism (e.g., Welsh Y Ddraig Goch).
