Strigoi

undead Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

Vampiric undead of Romanian folklore, distinguished from many other European vampire traditions by an active 'living' form (strigoi viu, a sorcerer or witch) and a 'dead' form (strigoi mort) that rises from the grave to torment family members.

Details

Origin:
Folk traditions of Romania, especially rural Wallachia, Moldavia, Transylvania, and Oltenia. Cognate beliefs extend across the Balkans and into the Slavic world.
Regions:
Transylvania, Wallachia, Moldavia, Oltenia, Banat
First Reported:
Pre-modern

Full Account

Strigoi are vampiric beings of Romanian folklore. Tradition distinguishes the strigoi viu — a living person, often described as a witch or sorcerer, identified at birth by features such as being born with a caul, with teeth, or as the seventh child of the same sex — and the strigoi mort, a deceased person who returns from the grave to harm the living, especially their own family. The strigoi mort is the type most directly continuous with the broader European vampire tradition: it is said to drink blood, drain vitality, and cause family members and livestock to weaken and die. Folklore holds that strigoi avoid garlic, onions, and incense, fear iron, and may be detected in the grave by uncorrupted flesh and a body posture suggesting recent activity. Defensive practices include exhumation followed by staking with hawthorn, oak, or ash; decapitation; burning of the heart; and reburial face-down. Although the tradition is ancient, it has remained living: in February 2004 family members in Marotinu de Sus, Dolj County, exhumed the corpse of Petre Toma, removed his heart, burned it, and mixed the ash with water for a sick relative — leading to criminal prosecution under Romanian law. Romanian strigoi traditions form part of the wider Balkan and Slavic vampire complex and are sometimes cited as a major influence on Bram Stoker's Dracula (1897).

Etymology

Origin:
Romanian, ultimately from Latin striga (witch) and strix (screech-owl), via long-attested Roman beliefs in nocturnal blood-drinking female demons.
Meaning:
Strigoi (masculine), strigoaică (feminine); the augmentative -oi denotes a large, dangerous form of the strigă.
First Use:
Documented in Romanian ethnographic literature from the 19th century; the underlying Latin striga is attested from antiquity

Cultural Significance

Strigoi are widely understood in Romanian folk religion as the spirits of the unbaptised, the unsettled dead, sorcerers, or those who lived sinful lives, returning to torment family. Garlic, onion, incense, and hawthorn provide protection; in serious cases, exhumation and staking or heart-burning are practised.
