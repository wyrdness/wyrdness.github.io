Overview

Duendes are small humanoid folkloric beings of Iberian, Latin American, and Filipino tradition, ranging from helpful or mischievous house-sprites to dangerous forest dwellers said to lure children astray; in 1933 Federico García Lorca repurposed the word as an aesthetic concept for the dark vital force of art.

Full Account

Duende (plural: duendes) is a class of small humanoid folkloric beings widespread in Iberian, Latin American, and Filipino tradition. The Spanish term derives from a contraction of dueño/dueñe de casa ('master/owner of the house'). In Spain, duendes are typically house-sprites or nature-spirits whose dispositions vary regionally: the trasgos enter through the chimney to move and steal household objects; the anjanas/xanas of Cantabria and Asturias are nymph-like water guardians; the busgosos are bearded forest-dwellers who play the flute for shepherds; the nuberos rule clouds and weather. In Latin America the duende takes on darker forms: the Tata Duende of Belize and Guatemala is a forest spirit who lacks thumbs and lures children into the bush, and Mexican folklore describes wall-dwelling duendes who attempt to clip children's toenails. In the Philippines, duwende inhabit rocks, caves, and termite-mound 'punsó', divided into white (benevolent) and black (malevolent) types, with the nunò sa punsó receiving special respect. In 1933 Federico García Lorca delivered the lecture 'Juego y teoría del duende' ('Play and Theory of the Duende'), reinterpreting duende as the dark, earthy, death-aware vital force at the root of flamenco and great art.

Etymology

Origin:
Spanish duende, contracted from dueño de casa ('master of the house') / duen de casa, ultimately from Latin dominus ('master')
Meaning:
Originally 'master of the house' (a household spirit); later generalized to a class of small humanoid folkloric beings
First Use:
Attested in Spanish folkloric texts from the medieval and early modern periods

Cultural Significance

Duendes are typically respected, propitiated, or warded against rather than confronted; offerings, polite speech, and avoidance of certain natural sites form the practical core of belief.
