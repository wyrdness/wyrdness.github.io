Drop Bear

urban_legend Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

A wholly fictional Australian creature: an oversized, predatory koala that supposedly drops from eucalyptus trees onto unsuspecting passers-by, used as a long-standing in-joke to prank tourists.

Details

Origin:
Joke localised to Australia, particularly its eucalyptus forests; told primarily to international tourists.
Regions:
All Australian states and territories (within the joke)
First Reported:
1967

Full Account

The drop bear is a deliberately fictitious Australian creature, used as a tongue-in-cheek tall tale told primarily to tourists and visiting service personnel. The joke describes a much larger, carnivorous version of a koala that ambushes prey by dropping from the eucalyptus canopy. Early documented appearances of 'drop bear' in print include a 1967 Army newspaper article, a 1976 Army article, a 1978 Royal Australian Navy News article, and a 1982 Canberra Times advertisement. The Australian Museum maintains a deliberately straight-faced entry classifying the drop bear as Thylarctos plummetus, describing a 120 kg arboreal predatory marsupial roughly the size of a leopard with coarse orange fur and broad premolars; a small museum display has accompanied the entry. Australian Geographic has run an April Fools' article on drop bears (2013), and a 2012 Australian Geographer paper, 'Indirect tracking of drop bears using GNSS technology', extended the joke into a peer-reviewed venue. Suggested 'protections' include placing forks in one's hair, smearing Vegemite or toothpaste behind the ears or in armpits, and speaking with an Australian accent. The drop bear belongs to the same humorous tradition as North American 'fearsome critters' (jackalope, hoop snake, splintercat) and in Terry Pratchett's The Last Continent (1998) the drop bears live on the parodic continent 'Fourecks'. Some commentators have drawn comparisons to the extinct Pleistocene marsupial Thylacoleo carnifex, which 2016 and 2018 paleontological studies indicated was a strong climber and likely ambush predator.

Cultural Significance

Australians broadly understand the drop bear as a joke; the joke itself is the cultural artifact.
