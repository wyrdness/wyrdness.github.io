Overview

Full Account

Astaroth is a major demon of Christian demonology. The name derives directly from the Hebrew Ashtoreth / Ashtaroth, the biblical name(s) for the West-Semitic goddess Astarte (Phoenician ʿAṯtart, Mesopotamian Ishtar), whose worship was condemned in the Hebrew Bible. Through the standard process of demonising the gods of rival pagan nations, this goddess was transformed by the early Christian period and the Middle Ages into a male demon. He appears in the Book of Abramelin (c. 1458), in Johann Weyer's Pseudomonarchia Daemonum (1577) as 'a great and mighty duke' commanding forty legions, and as the 29th spirit of the Ars Goetia in the Lesser Key of Solomon, where he is described as 'a Mighty, Strong Duke' who 'appeareth in the Form of an hurtful Angel riding on an Infernal Beast like a Dragon, and carrying in his right hand a Viper'. He teaches the liberal sciences, answers questions about the past, present, and future, and gives the secrets of how the angels fell. In Sebastien Michaelis's hierarchy of demons (1612), Astaroth is a Prince of the Order of Thrones who tempts through laziness, vanity, and rationalised philosophies and whose adversary is St Bartholomew. In Collin de Plancy's Dictionnaire Infernal (1818), he is depicted as 'a very ugly angel' riding a dragon-like beast.

Etymology

Origin:
From the Hebrew Ashtoreth / Ashtaroth, denoting the West-Semitic goddess Astarte (Phoenician ʿAṯtart). The biblical Hebrew form was deliberately vocalised with the vowels of bōšeṯ ('shame') as a polemical device.
Meaning:
Originally the name of a Phoenician goddess of love, war, and fertility; demonised in Christian tradition.
First Use:
Biblical Hebrew (1 Kings, 2 Kings, etc.); demonological use from the early Middle Ages; explicit demonic characterisation in Ælfric of Eynsham (d. 1010) and elaborated in 15th–17th c. grimoiric literature.

  • 1275-01-01 — Jacobus de Voragine includes Astaroth in the Golden Legend (Wide medieval circulation of the figure)
  • 1458-01-01 — The Book of Abramelin (manuscript) treats Astaroth as one of the eight sub-princes (Major late-medieval grimoiric reference)
  • 1577-01-01 — Johann Weyer's Pseudomonarchia Daemonum lists Astaroth as a great duke commanding forty legions (Standard early-modern reference)

  • 1636-01-01 — Urban Grandier's alleged diabolical pact (Loudun) includes Astaroth's signature (Notorious historical case linking Astaroth to early-modern witch trials)
  • 1801-01-01 — Francis Barrett's The Magus reprints Astaroth's attributes (Bridge to the 19th-century occult revival)
  • 1818-01-01 — Collin de Plancy's Dictionnaire Infernal popularises the modern image of Astaroth (Establishes the modern iconographic image)
  • 1904-01-01 — S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Aleister Crowley publish The Goetia (Lesser Key of Solomon) (Standard modern grimoiric edition placing Astaroth as duke no. 29)

Cultural Significance

In Christian demonology, Astaroth functioned as a personification of false philosophy and pagan religion, whose temptations the faithful were to resist.
