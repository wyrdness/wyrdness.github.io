Ceasg

mythological_creature Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

A mermaid figure of Scottish Gaelic folklore: the upper body of a woman joined to the tail of a grilse (young salmon). Captured ceasgs are said to grant three wishes in exchange for their freedom.

Details

Origin:
Coastal and riverine Scottish Gàidhealtachd (Highlands and Western Isles).
Regions:
Scottish Highlands, Outer Hebrides, Inner Hebrides, Argyll, West Coast of Scotland

1900

Full Account

The ceasg is a mermaid-like creature of Scottish Gaelic Highland and Hebridean folklore. She has the upper body of a beautiful young woman joined to the tail of a grilse (a young salmon), and inhabits both salt water and the rivers and streams that the salmon ascends. Tradition holds that a fisherman who captures a ceasg without injuring her will be granted three wishes for her release. In some stories ceasgs marry mortal men and bear children; several Highland sea-pilots and seafaring families are traditionally said to descend from such unions, and the ceasg-ancestress is sometimes believed to protect her descendants at sea. Other tales are less benign — one widely retold story has a ceasg swallow a hero who survives within her until charmed forth by harp music. The folklorist Donald A. Mackenzie suggested that the ceasg may preserve a memory of an older sea-goddess to whom human sacrifices were offered. The figure is recorded in 19th- and early-20th-century Scottish Gaelic folklore collections, including those of John Gregorson Campbell.

Also Known As

  • Maighdean na tuinne (Scottish Gaelic)
  • Maighdean mhara (Scottish Gaelic)

Etymology

Origin:
Scottish Gaelic ceasg, used in Highland tradition for this mermaid figure.
Meaning:
The Gaelic word ceasg refers to the mermaid figure; the descriptive epithets maighdean na tuinne and maighdean mhara mean 'maid of the wave' and 'maid of the sea' respectively.
First Use:
Recorded in Scottish Gaelic oral tradition; published English-language attestations from the 19th century.
