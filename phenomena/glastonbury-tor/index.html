Glastonbury Tor

Overview

A 158-metre (518-foot) conical hill near Glastonbury, Somerset, England, topped by the roofless 14th-century tower of St Michael's Church. Long associated with Arthurian Avalon, with Celtic Otherworld traditions, and with modern earth-mysteries and pilgrimage culture; now a National Trust property.

Full Account

Glastonbury Tor is a conical hill rising 158 metres (518 feet) above the Somerset Levels at coordinates 51°08′40″N 2°41′55″W. Geologically, it is formed of Jurassic Bridport Sand Formation strata that act as a caprock protecting underlying limestone and clay. Its slopes are cut by seven prominent terraces (lynchets) of disputed origin; explanations include medieval agricultural use, defensive ramparts, a pilgrimage spiral, or a three-dimensional labyrinth (a hypothesis advanced from 1968). Archaeological excavation by Philip Rahtz between 1964 and 1966 confirmed Dark Age (5th–7th century) occupation including post-holes, hearths, north-south burials, Mediterranean amphora fragments, and a hollow bronze head, indicating high-status Christian or hermitic activity. A timber church dedicated to St Michael was built on the summit in the 11th or 12th century. It was destroyed by an earthquake on 11 September 1275 — an event recorded across England by contemporary chroniclers and confirmed in modern seismological reviews. A second stone church was built in the 14th century by Abbot Adam of Sodbury; only its tower survived the Dissolution of the Monasteries in 1539, when the last Abbot of Glastonbury, Richard Whiting, and two monks were hanged, drawn and quartered at the Tor's summit. Medieval and modern legend identifies the Tor with Avalon (Gerald of Wales, c. 1191–1216), with the supposed 1191 'discovery' of King Arthur and Guinevere's grave at Glastonbury Abbey. In Celtic-Welsh tradition the Tor is associated with Gwyn ap Nudd, lord of the Otherworld (Annwn). The site is central to a wide range of modern esoteric traditions, including Katharine Maltwood's 1927 'Glastonbury Zodiac' theory, John Michell's ley-line writings, and contemporary Goddess-movement and pagan pilgrimage. The National Trust acquired the Tor in 1937 (after Robert Neville-Grenville's bequest); it now welcomes hundreds of thousands of visitors annually.

Etymology

Origin:
'Tor' is a West Country English word for a high rocky hill, from Old English torr. The town name 'Glastonbury' comes from Old English Glæstingaburg, the stronghold of the Glæstingas.
Meaning:
Glastonbury Tor — 'the hill above the stronghold of the Glæstingas'.
First Use:
'Glastonbury' attested in Old English from the 8th–10th century

Cultural Significance

The Tor is widely regarded as a sacred hill — Christian, pagan, and Arthurian — and as a focal point for the modern Goddess movement. Beliefs range from devout Christian pilgrimage and Anglican practice to Goddess-and-God pagan rite, druidic ceremony, and secular cultural tourism.

Related Phenomena

  • Sedona vortexes — Modern 'energy site' analogue in the United States
