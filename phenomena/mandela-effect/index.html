Mandela Effect

psychic_phenomena Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

A phenomenon in which large numbers of people share a confidently held but factually incorrect memory. Named by Fiona Broome in 2009 after the widespread false memory of Nelson Mandela having died in prison in the 1980s — Mandela was in fact released in 1990 and died in 2013.

Details

Origin:
Reported globally wherever there is a shared culture and a strong online discussion community; most documentation is in English-language media.
Regions:
Internet-mediated cultural sphere
First Reported:
2009

Full Account

The Mandela Effect is the popular name for a category of shared false memories — situations in which large groups of people remember the same incorrect detail of a public fact, brand, or media work. The term was coined in 2009 by paranormal researcher Fiona Broome, who at Dragon Con discovered that a number of other attendees shared her vivid false memory of Nelson Mandela having died in prison in the 1980s; Mandela was actually released in 1990, served as President of South Africa from 1994 to 1999, and died on 5 December 2013. Recurring examples include the Berenstain Bears (often misremembered as 'Berenstein'), the Monopoly Man (often falsely recalled as wearing a monocle), Darth Vader's line in The Empire Strikes Back (often misremembered as 'Luke, I am your father' rather than 'No, I am your father'), and a non-existent 1990s film called 'Shazaam' supposedly starring Sinbad as a genie. Mainstream cognitive psychology — drawing on the work of Elizabeth Loftus and others — interprets the phenomenon as a familiar effect of constructive memory, source-monitoring error, the misinformation effect, and social transmission of error, sharpened by the internet age. A small minority of commentators, beginning with Broome, propose paranormal explanations such as parallel universes or timeline shifts.

Also Known As

  • Collective false memory (English)
  • Shared confabulation (English)

Etymology

Origin:
Coined by paranormal researcher Fiona Broome at Dragon Con in 2009 (article published 2010) following discussion of widely shared false memories of Nelson Mandela dying in prison.
Meaning:
A 'Mandela Effect' is a vivid, confidently held memory of a fact, event, or detail that turns out to differ from documented reality, particularly when the same false memory is shared by many people.
First Use:
2009 (oral, Dragon Con); written use widely from 2010 onward.

Timeline

  • 1974-01-01 — Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer publish 'Reconstruction of Automobile Destruction', a foundational study of misinformation effects on memory (Establishes scientific framework later used to explain Mandela Effect cases)
  • 2009-01-01 — Fiona Broome coins 'Mandela Effect' at Dragon Con and shortly afterward online (Names the phenomenon)
  • 2013-12-05 — Death of Nelson Mandela at age 95, refocusing attention on the false 1980s prison-death memory (Renews discussion of the original namesake case)
  • 2015-01-01 — Viral Reddit and Tumblr discussion of the 'Berenstain Bears' / 'Berenstein Bears' spelling popularises the term (Brings the Mandela Effect into mainstream internet culture)
  • 2016-01-01 — Mainstream press coverage of Mandela Effect cases, often associated with parallel-universe and CERN-related conspiracy theories (Cements the term in popular use)

Cultural Significance

In paranormal-leaning communities, Mandela Effect cases are often treated as evidence of reality-shifts; in mainstream culture they are usually treated as curiosities of memory.
