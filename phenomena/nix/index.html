Nix

fae_folklore Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

A shapeshifting water spirit of Germanic and Scandinavian folklore that lures victims with enchanting music — often violin — and drowns them, with regional male and female forms.

Details

Origin:
Germanic and Nordic Europe, including England (as the Old English nicor and its descendants), Germany, the Low Countries, Scandinavia, Finland, and Estonia.
Regions:
Germanic Europe, Scandinavia, Baltic
First Reported:
Early medieval

Full Account

The Nix (German Nix masc., Nixe fem.; English nixie; Swedish näck; Norwegian/Danish nøkk; Finnish näkki; Old English nicor) is a Germanic and Scandinavian water spirit of rivers, lakes, and waterfalls. Most often a male spirit in the Nordic tradition and a more mermaid-like figure in German tradition, the nix is fundamentally a shapeshifter — appearing as a young man near water, an old bearded fiddler, a beautiful woman, a fish, a snake, or most famously the 'brook horse' (bäckahäst), a white or black horse that lures riders to drown. The nix's defining attribute is irresistible music, generally played on the violin; tradition holds that those who can win his teaching gain extraordinary musical skill, but at the price of grave spiritual danger. Defences include throwing steel or iron into the water, sticking a knife in the ground before swimming, calling the nix by his true name, making the sign of the cross, and offerings such as three drops of blood or a black animal. Major sources include Jacob Grimm's Deutsche Mythologie (1835), the medieval Nibelungenlied, and 19th-century Nordic visual depictions by Theodor Kittelsen and Ernst Josephson.

Etymology

Origin:
From Proto-Germanic *nikwus / *nikwis(i), ultimately from Proto-Indo-European *neigʷ- ('to wash')
Meaning:
A water spirit; the English 'nixie' is from German Nixe (feminine)
First Use:
Old English nicor attested in early medieval texts; modern English 'nixie' borrowed via German in the 19th century

Timeline

Cultural Significance

The nix is treated across the Germanic world as a real water-occupant requiring respect and ritual precaution.
