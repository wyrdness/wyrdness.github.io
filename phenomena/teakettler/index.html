Teakettler

cryptid Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

A small, behaviorally defined fearsome critter of North American lumberjack folklore that produces a teakettle-like whistling sound. First catalogued in William T. Cox's 1910 'Fearsome Creatures of the Lumberwoods'.

Details

Origin:
Folklore current in the lumber-camp belt of the northern United States and Canada in the late 19th and early 20th century.
Regions:
Great Lakes states, New England, Pacific Northwest, Eastern Canada
First Reported:
1910

Full Account

The Teakettler is one of the 'fearsome critters' of North American lumberjack folklore — a class of comic, tall-tale creatures invented and elaborated by loggers in the late 19th and early 20th centuries to entertain new camp recruits and to explain unusual forest sounds. The Teakettler is described, in William T. Cox's 'Fearsome Creatures of the Lumberwoods, With a Few Desert and Mountain Beasts' (1910), as a small variety of forest vermin that makes a noise like a boiling teakettle. Compared with most fearsome critters, it is notable for being defined almost entirely by its behavior rather than its appearance, and for being among the genuinely 'minor' figures in the canon. The creature also appears in Jorge Luis Borges's 'Book of Imaginary Beings' (1969), where it is treated as a representative example of North American lumberjack tall-tales.

Also Known As

  • Teakettler (U.S. and Canadian lumber camps)

Etymology

Origin:
Coined in early 20th-century American lumberjack slang from 'teakettle' + agentive suffix '-er'.
Meaning:
A creature that 'teakettles' — i.e. produces a kettle-like whistling sound.
First Use:
Documented in print in William T. Cox's 'Fearsome Creatures of the Lumberwoods' (1910).

Timeline

Cultural Significance

The Teakettler is straightforwardly understood as a comic invention used to entertain newcomers to a logging camp, not as a sincere creature claim.
