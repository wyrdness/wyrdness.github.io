Yamauba

entity_spirit Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

A morally ambivalent yokai in Japanese folklore — an elderly mountain woman who may shelter and aid travelers or devour them. She is most famous as the mother of the folk hero Kintaro and is a major subject of Noh theater. She embodies the dual nature of the wild mountain: nurturing yet consuming, maternal yet dangerous.

Details

Origin:
Found throughout Japanese folklore, with documented regional variants across Honshu, Hokkaido, Shikoku, and Kyushu. Particularly associated with the mountainous interior of Japan and mountain-pass routes historically traveled by merchants and ox-drivers.
Regions:
Throughout Japan, Miyazaki Prefecture (yamahime variant), Hokkaido (protective maternal variant), Shikoku, Kyushu, Kagawa Prefecture (kawajoro river variant), Kochi Prefecture (deified household protective variant)
First Reported:
1400-01-01

Full Account

Yamauba (山姥, 'mountain hag') is one of the most complex and richly documented yokai in the Japanese supernatural tradition. She typically appears as a monstrous elderly woman with long, disheveled white or gold-white hair and filthy, tattered clothing, dwelling deep in the mountains far from human settlement. Her most disturbing physical trait — appearing in some traditions — is a hidden second mouth on the crown of her head, concealed beneath her hair, which she feeds using her hair as tendrils. Yamauba's behavior is fundamentally ambivalent: she sometimes provides travelers with shelter, food, and guidance; at other times she is a cannibalistic predator who devours those who accept her hospitality or fall into her power. She can shapeshift, most notably into a beautiful young woman to lure victims. Her only documented supernatural weakness in some accounts is a specific flower containing her soul. In some traditions she exhibits superhuman strength, reportedly catching bullets with her bare hands. Regional variants proliferate across Japan: in Miyazaki Prefecture, a yamahime (mountain princess) sings beautifully while grooming; in Hokkaido, Shikoku, and Kyushu she is sometimes a protective maternal figure; in Kagawa Prefecture river variants called kawajoro (river ladies) exist; in Kochi Prefecture she is deified as a household protective deity. Her most culturally significant role is as the mother of Kintaro (Golden Boy), a folk hero of superhuman strength who grew up in the mountains under her care and later became the legendary warrior Sakata no Kintoki. Yamauba also appears prominently in classical theater: the Noh play Yamauba, composed by Konparu Zenchiku, depicts her as an ancient mountain fairy who has decorated the earth since the beginning of creation. In Noh, she is a figure of cosmic significance rather than a simple monster.

Etymology

Origin:
Japanese; compound of yama (山, mountain) and uba (姥, old woman, hag, or wet nurse). The wet-nurse meaning of uba connects to her role as Kintaro's mountain mother.
Meaning:
Mountain hag or mountain old woman; the term also carries the meaning of mountain wet-nurse through the uba component.
First Use:
The Noh play Yamauba (by Konparu Zenchiku, c. 14th–15th century) is among the earliest literary treatments; the oral tradition predates it

Timeline

  • 1400-01-01 — Noh play Yamauba composed by Konparu Zenchiku; establishes the theatrical tradition depicting yamauba as a cosmic mountain fairy (Most important early literary treatment; shapes the figure's cultural reception through to the present)

Cultural Significance

Yamauba represents the mountain as a fundamentally ambivalent space: nurturing and dangerous simultaneously. She is the wild feminine in its most uncontrolled form — maternal (raising Kintaro, providing shelter) and consuming (devouring travelers). The hidden mouth is a recurring symbol of her dangerous hidden nature. In the Noh tradition, she is not merely a monster but a cosmic figure, ancient and tied to the fundamental cycles of the natural world.

Related Phenomena
