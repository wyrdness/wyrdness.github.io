Nine-Tailed Fox

shapeshifter Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

A powerful fox spirit of East Asian mythology, distinguished by nine tails. It can live for centuries, accumulate spiritual power, and shapeshift into a beautiful human — beneficent in early Chinese tradition, often seductive and dangerous in later accounts.

Full Account

The nine-tailed fox (Chinese 九尾狐 jiǔwěihú; Korean 구미호 gumiho; Japanese 九尾の狐 kyūbi no kitsune; Vietnamese hồ ly tinh) is one of the most influential supernatural figures of East Asian mythology. The earliest reference appears in the Shanhaijing (Classic of Mountains and Seas), which describes a nine-tailed fox in the land of Qingqiu (青丘). In early Chinese tradition the nine-tailed fox was an auspicious omen associated with rulership and harmony; later, particularly from the Han onwards, it shifted toward an ambivalent and then frequently malevolent figure — a spiritually advanced fox capable of shapeshifting into a beautiful woman to bewitch and harm men. Scholar Guo Pu (276–324 CE) systematised the idea that foxes acquire transformative powers with age (50, 100, 1000 years), and the celestial nine-tailed fox is described in the Tang-dynasty Youyang Zazu as serving in the Palace of the Sun and Moon. Korean tradition (gumiho) typically frames the nine-tailed fox as a dangerous female spirit, while modern Japanese tradition incorporates the kitsune complex and the Tamamo-no-Mae legend. The figure remains massively present in contemporary East Asian and global popular culture.

Cultural Significance

The nine-tailed fox is variously seen as auspicious omen, dangerous seductress, demonic temptress, and celestial servant, depending on tradition and era.

Related Phenomena

  • Kitsune — Japanese fox spirit complex of which the nine-tailed kyūbi no kitsune is the most powerful form
  • Huli jing — Chinese fox spirit class; the nine-tailed fox is its highest form
  • Gumiho — Korean nine-tailed fox tradition
