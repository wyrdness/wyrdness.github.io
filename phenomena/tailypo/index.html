Tailypo

cryptid Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

An Appalachian folktale creature, dog- or cat-like with a long tail, that returns night after night to a hunter who cut off and ate its tail, demanding its return and ultimately killing him.

Details

Origin:
Folktale concentrated in the Appalachian region and the broader American South. The tale also appears, with variations, in African-American Southern oral tradition and in 20th-century children's-literature retellings.
Regions:
Appalachia, American South
First Reported:
Late 19th–early 20th century

Full Account

The Tailypo is a creature of North American folklore, particularly Appalachian and Southern oral tradition. In the standard tale, an isolated hunter or hermit shoots an unfamiliar animal in his cabin and severs its long tail, which he then cooks and eats. That night, and on subsequent nights, the creature returns to scratch at the cabin and call (variously) 'Tailypo, tailypo, all I want is my tailypo' or 'Who's got my tailypo?' The hunter's three hounds (often named in regional variants 'Uno, Ino, and Cumptico-Calico') drive the creature off on the first nights but are killed or scattered one by one, until on the final night the creature breaks into the cabin and kills the hunter. The Tailypo itself is described as roughly dog-sized, dark-furred, with yellow or red eyes, pointed bobcat-like ears, and a long thin tail; despite an animal appearance, it speaks plainly. Folklorists treat the Tailypo as a cautionary story about isolation, theft, and disrespect for wild creatures rather than as a cryptozoological claim.

Also Known As

  • Taily-po (Appalachia, United States)
  • Tailipoe (American South)

Etymology

Origin:
Appalachian English; the name imitates the creature's repeated call 'tailypo' for its severed tail. Spellings vary widely (Tailypo, Tailipoe, Taily-po) reflecting oral transmission.
Meaning:
An onomatopoeic / dialect form of 'tail' used in the creature's refrain.
First Use:
Oral tradition; the tale is widely cited in 20th-century Appalachian and African-American folktale collections.

Cultural Significance

The Tailypo is not generally treated as a real animal, but as a frightening cautionary tale used in cabin and campfire storytelling, in which violation of an unfamiliar creature's body brings disproportionate retribution.
