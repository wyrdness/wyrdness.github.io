Basket Woman

entity_spirit Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

A giant, child-stealing ogress of Pacific Northwest Coast Indigenous folklore, said to carry off careless or disobedient children in a large pack basket on her back. Best known by the Kwakwaka'wakw name Dzunukwa.

Details

Origin:
Coastal and forested regions of the Pacific Northwest, from southeastern Alaska through coastal British Columbia and Washington into Oregon and northern California, in the territories of Northwest Coast nations.
Regions:
British Columbia coast, Vancouver Island, Pacific Northwest, Olympic Peninsula, Oregon Coast
First Reported:
Pre-contact

Full Account

Basket Woman, also called the Basket Ogress, is a recurring figure in the oral traditions of many Pacific Northwest Coast nations, including the Kwakwaka'wakw (Kwakiutl), Heiltsuk, Tlingit, Coast Salish, and others. She is most famously known by the Kwakwaka'wakw name Dzunukwa (Tsonoqua), and on the Oregon coast as Asin. She is described as a tall, dark, naked, and wild-haired giantess who roams the forest with an enormous cedar-bark or root pack basket on her back. Children who wander, disobey, or stay out after dark are caught and carried off in the basket to be eaten. In most stories the children outwit her, often because she is sleepy or dim-witted. In Kwakwaka'wakw ceremonial life Dzunukwa is also a more complex figure: a wealth-bringing being whose mask appears in the Tlasala (Peace) and other dance complexes, with deep clan and potlatch significance. Conflating the bogeyman role with the ceremonial role flattens an important cultural distinction.

Etymology

Origin:
English 'Basket Woman' / 'Basket Ogress' is a 19th–20th century anthropological gloss for a cluster of related Northwest Coast figures; Indigenous names include Kwak'wala Dzunukwa (Tsonoqua) and Alsea/regional Asin.
Meaning:
Dzunukwa is sometimes glossed as 'Wild Woman of the Woods'; the English designations describe her primary attribute, the large pack basket in which she abducts children.
First Use:
Recorded in English-language ethnography in the late 19th century by Franz Boas and George Hunt

Cultural Significance

Across Northwest Coast traditions, the Basket Woman is understood as a real being inhabiting the deep forest. She is fearsome but defeatable, and in some traditions her defeat brings wealth and clan honor.
