Redcap

fae_folklore Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

A murderous goblin of Anglo-Scottish Border folklore who haunts ruined peel-towers and castles, dyeing his cap in the blood of travellers he kills.

Details

Origin:
Confined to the Anglo-Scottish Border country, with sympathetic legends in adjacent areas of northern England and southern Scotland.
Regions:
Scottish Borders, Northumberland, Cumbria, Liddesdale, Tweeddale
First Reported:
1802-01-01

Full Account

The Redcap is a malevolent fae of the Anglo-Scottish Border country, particularly associated with ruined peel-towers and border castles. Sir Walter Scott describes him in the Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border (1802), and William Henderson preserves the canonical description in Notes on the Folk-Lore of the Northern Counties of England and the Borders (1879): a short, thickset old man with long prominent teeth, skinny taloned fingers, large fiery red eyes, grizzled hair streaming down his shoulders, iron boots, a pikestaff in his left hand, and a red cap on his head. He is said to be impossible to overcome by mortal strength, but flees and drops a tooth when confronted with scripture or the sign of the cross. The most famous Redcap of legend is Robin Redcap, the supposed familiar of the 14th-century Lord William de Soulis at Hermitage Castle in Liddesdale.

Etymology

Origin:
From English red and cap, named for the cap which the creature is said to dye in the blood of travellers it kills. Powrie and dunter are Scots dialect names for the same being, dunter from the dunting (dull-thudding) sounds said to be heard within the haunted towers.
Meaning:
'Red cap' — referring to the blood-dyed cap that is the creature's defining attribute.
First Use:
Sir Walter Scott's Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border (1802) is the earliest widely-cited literary record of the named figure; older Border tradition is implied but undated.

Cultural Significance

Within Border tradition, Redcaps were taken seriously as a real supernatural hazard of ruins; the iconography of cap, pike, and iron boots is consistent across recorded variants.

Related Phenomena

  • Powrie — Scots Border name for closely related (or identical) goblin
  • Dunter — Border name emphasising the muffled noise made in haunted towers
  • Brownie — Benevolent household fae of British folklore, structural opposite of the Redcap
  • Kobold — Germanic goblin with overlapping motifs
  • Goblin — Wider European class of malevolent fae to which Redcap belongs
  • Red Caps in chapbooks — Diverse cap-wearing fae figures (e.g. Cornish red caps) sometimes conflated with Border Redcap
