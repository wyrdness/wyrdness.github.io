Nurikabe

entity_spirit Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

A Japanese yōkai of folklore from northern Kyushu that manifests as an invisible barrier blocking the path of nighttime travelers. Defeating it traditionally requires sweeping a stick along its base.

Details

Origin:
Indigenous folk tradition is concentrated in northern Kyushu, especially Fukuoka Prefecture (former Chikuzen Province) and Ōita Prefecture. The figure is now nationally and internationally recognised through Mizuki Shigeru's manga.
Regions:
Kyushu, Fukuoka Prefecture, Ōita Prefecture, Onga County (former Chikuzen Province)
First Reported:
c. 1660

Full Account

Nurikabe (塗壁, ぬりかべ, 'plastered wall') is a yōkai documented in folk traditions of northern Kyushu — particularly Onga County in former Chikuzen Province (modern Fukuoka Prefecture) and adjacent parts of Ōita Prefecture. It manifests as an invisible obstruction that suddenly bars a traveler's path on a road at night: attempts to walk around or above it fail, since the wall extends as needed. Folklorist Kunio Yanagita's 1938 collection records that 'when one is walking a path at night, suddenly the destination one is going towards turns into a wall'. The traditional remedy, recorded in the same regional sources, is to sweep a stick along the bottom of the wall — striking the top has no effect. Until 2007 the creature was thought to lack any pre-modern visual representation; that year, a c. 1660 yōkai handscroll held by Brigham Young University was identified as containing a labelled illustration of nurikabe shown as a three-eyed, dog-elephant-like beast, which is now considered the earliest extant depiction. The modern, beloved visual identity of nurikabe — a great rectangular wall with arms, legs and a face — was created by manga artist Mizuki Shigeru, who said the design grew out of a personal experience of an invisible obstruction during the Pacific War in New Guinea. Through Mizuki's GeGeGe no Kitarō series the character became a national icon. Skeptics and some cultural commentators interpret the original folk experience as a sleep-deprivation, fog or ground-hugging mist phenomenon known to nighttime travelers in the Kyushu countryside.

Etymology

Origin:
Japanese: 塗 (nuru, 'to plaster, paint, smear') + 壁 (kabe, 'wall')
Meaning:
'Plastered wall' or 'painted wall'; also rendered 塗り壁
First Use:
Recorded in 20th-century Kyushu folklore collections (notably Yanagita Kunio, 1938); the BYU c. 1660 scroll labels its creature 'nurikabe', placing the term in the mid-Edo period

Timeline

  • c. 1660 — Earliest known illustrated record: a yōkai handscroll labelled with the name 'nurikabe', later acquired by the L. Tom Perry Special Collections at Brigham Young University (Pushes documented existence of the figure to the mid-Edo period)
  • 1938 — Folklorist Kunio Yanagita publishes accounts of nurikabe in folk-tale collections, locating it in Onga County, Fukuoka (Establishes the canonical folkloric narrative)
  • 1968 — Mizuki Shigeru's GeGeGe no Kitarō introduces the iconic anthropomorphic wall design (Modern visual identity of nurikabe; the character becomes a member of Kitarō's troupe)
  • 2007 — Yumoto Kōichi identifies the c. 1660 BYU scroll's creature as a labelled nurikabe (First confirmed pre-modern image of the yōkai)

Cultural Significance

Tradition interprets nighttime obstruction on lonely roads as a yōkai attack rather than mere disorientation; the stick-sweeping remedy is canonical.

Related Phenomena

  • Nobiagari — Yōkai that grows taller and taller before a traveler, similarly obstructive
  • Betobeto-san — Invisible road yōkai that follows night travelers
  • Tanuki — In some regional accounts, mischievous tanuki are blamed for the same disorienting nighttime experiences
  • Nurarihyon — Similarly known mainly through Edo emaki and revived by Mizuki Shigeru
