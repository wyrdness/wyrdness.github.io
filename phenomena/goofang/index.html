Full Account

The Goofang is a fantastical fish from the tall tale tradition of North American lumberjack camps, belonging to the category of creatures known as 'fearsome critters.' According to the tradition, the Goofang swims exclusively backward — tail first — to keep water out of its eyes. This is its sole defining characteristic. The creature was described as 'about the size of a sunfish, only larger' in Charles E. Brown's Paul Bunyan Natural History (1935), which documented the fictional wildlife associated with Paul Bunyan's logging camps. The Goofang is also mentioned by Jorge Luis Borges in his Book of Imaginary Beings (1957) with the same description. Like other fearsome critters, the Goofang originated as entertainment in Great Lakes region logging camps of the late 19th and early 20th centuries, serving to amuse workers and haze newcomers. The fearsome critters tradition was first documented in print by William T. Cox in Fearsome Creatures of the Lumberwoods (1910), published by Judd & Detweiler, Washington D.C., with illustrations and Latin classifications by George B. Sudworth. Henry H. Tryon published a later collection, Fearsome Critters, in 1939.

Cultural Significance

The fearsome critters, including the Goofang, were part of a conscious tradition of humorous invention in lumberjack culture. They served as entertainment and social bonding tools in isolated logging camps, and as hazing mechanisms for newcomers ('greenhorns').
