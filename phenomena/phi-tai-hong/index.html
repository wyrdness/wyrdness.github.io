Phi Tai Hong

ghost_haunting Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

A vengeful ghost in Thai folklore arising from a person who died suddenly or violently — by murder, accident, drowning, suicide, or childbirth — and was unable to perform proper funeral rites, leaving the spirit angry and unwilling to depart.

Details

Origin:
Thailand and culturally adjacent Tai-speaking regions of mainland Southeast Asia.
Regions:
Mainland Southeast Asia
First Reported:
Ayutthaya period (17th century)

Full Account

Phi Tai Hong (Thai: ผีตายโหง) is one of the most feared classes of ghost in Thai supernatural belief. The compound 'tai hong' refers to dying an unnatural and violent death — by murder, drowning, accident, suicide, or other sudden cause — and the term emphasizes both the great suffering and the unexpectedness of the death. Such a death is held to prevent the spirit from passing peacefully to the next existence; instead it remains earthbound, full of resentment and unfulfilled desire, and may seek vengeance on those it holds responsible. Phi Tai Hong are described as especially dangerous in the first seven days after death, when they are most active and may try to kill others in the same manner they themselves died. The most violent variant is Tai Thang Klom — the ghost of a pregnant woman who died together with her unborn child — which is regarded as carrying the power of two spirits at once. Belief in Phi Tai Hong is rooted in Theravāda Buddhist concerns about the importance of proper death rites and a peaceful state of mind at the moment of dying. The figure has become a staple of Thai horror cinema, comics, and television.

Etymology

Origin:
Thai. 'Phi' (ผี) = ghost; 'tai' (ตาย) = to die; 'hong' (โหง) is glossed as combining the senses of great suffering and suddenness/unexpectedness.
Meaning:
'The ghost that died unexpectedly and violently'.
First Use:
Traditional Thai usage; documented at least since the Ayutthaya period via 17th-century European observers.

Timeline

  • 17th century — Jeremias van Vliet documents beliefs about violent-death spirits in Ayutthaya, including reported practices of burying pregnant women alive beneath fortifications to create protective spirits (Earliest European-language documentation of Thai bad-death ghost traditions)
  • 1999-04-01 — Release of Nonzee Nimibutr's film 'Nang Nak' (Modern blockbuster retelling of the Mae Nak legend — the archetypal Tai Thang Klom — re-popularizing Phi Tai Hong in Thai cinema)
  • 2003-10-08 — Release of 'Buppah Rahtree', a Thai comedy-horror centered on a Phi Tai Hong haunting (Introduces the figure to a new generation; spawns sequels)
  • 2010 — Release of the Thai film 'Tai Hong' ('Still') (Anthology film built explicitly around violent-death ghosts)

Cultural Significance

A violent or sudden death without proper rites produces a dangerous spirit that must be appeased through Buddhist merit-making and ritual.

Related Phenomena

  • Onryō — Japanese vengeful ghost arising from violent or wronged death
