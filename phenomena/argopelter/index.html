Argopelter

cryptid Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

A fearsome critter of North American lumberjack folklore — a slender, ape-like beast said to dwell in hollow trees of the conifer woods and to hurl branches and wood splinters at passing humans.

Details

Origin:
Said by Cox (1910) to inhabit the coniferous forests of North America from Maine to Oregon, with the densest reporting in the Great Lakes / Upper Midwest lumber region.
Regions:
Maine, Upper Midwest (Wisconsin, Minnesota, Michigan), Pacific Northwest (Oregon, Washington), Boreal and northern conifer forests
First Reported:
1910-01-01

Full Account

The Argopelter (also spelled Agropelter) is one of the so-called 'fearsome critters' of early-20th-century North American lumber-camp folklore. Documented most prominently in William T. Cox's Fearsome Creatures of the Lumberwoods (1910), the Argopelter was said to inhabit hollow trees in the coniferous forests stretching from Maine through the Great Lakes region and across to Oregon. The creature was described by Cox as having a slender, wiry body, the face of an ape, and arms 'like muscular whiplashes' which it used to snap off dead branches and hurl them with great force at intruders. Like other fearsome critters, the Argopelter served as a humorous folk explanation for hazards of the working forest — falling branches, mysterious injuries, and odd noises in the woods — and was often invoked to haze new lumberjacks. The Argopelter was reprinted and elaborated in Henry H. Tryon's Fearsome Critters (1939) and remains one of the more frequently cited members of the lumber-woods bestiary.

Also Known As

  • Agropelter (North American lumber camps)
  • Anthrocephalus craniofractens

Etymology

Origin:
Coined by William T. Cox in Fearsome Creatures of the Lumberwoods (1910); the prefix 'agro-' or 'argo-' is fanciful, while 'pelter' denotes the creature's habit of pelting passersby with wood.
Meaning:
Roughly 'one that pelts'; mock binomial Anthrocephalus craniofractens may be glossed 'human-headed skull-breaker'.
First Use:
1910 (William T. Cox).

Cultural Significance

The Argopelter was understood as a humorous fiction even by its tellers; fearsome critters served social functions of bonding and explanation rather than belief.
