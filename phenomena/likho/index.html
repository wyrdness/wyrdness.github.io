Likho

Overview

Likho is the Slavic personification of bad luck and evil fate, most often depicted as a one-eyed gaunt old woman in black or, more rarely, as a male forest goblin. It is a fairy-tale figure rather than a major mythological deity, woven into proverbs warning against tempting fate.

Likho is a figure of East and West Slavic folklore, most prominent in Russian, Ukrainian, Belarusian, and Polish tradition. The figure is woven into proverbs and fairy tales rather than tied to a specific landscape.

Full Account

Likho (Russian and Ukrainian Лихо, Belarusian лі́ха, Polish licho) is the Slavic embodiment of evil fate and misfortune. It appears in East Slavic and West Slavic fairy tales and proverbs as a one-eyed creature — typically an emaciated old woman dressed in black, occasionally an evil male goblin of the forest — whose touch or company brings ill luck. The word itself is a common noun meaning 'bad luck' or 'evil' in modern Russian and Ukrainian, and its root in Old Russian carried the sense of 'excessive' or 'odd', from which the pejorative use developed. Unlike major Slavic mythological figures such as Perun or Veles, Likho is not part of any canonical pantheon; instead it survives in folklore through cautionary tales and idioms (the Russian and Ukrainian proverb 'Don't wake Likho while it is quiet'). In one well-known fairy tale a blacksmith encounters Likho in a hut deep in the forest; she demands he forge her a new eye, but the smith blinds her remaining eye instead and escapes. Other narratives warn that Likho clings to its victims and cannot be drowned, and that one can only be rid of her by passing the curse to another.

Etymology

Origin:
Common Slavic noun meaning 'evil' or 'misfortune'. In Old Russian likho meant 'excessive', 'too much', 'remaining' or 'odd' (as opposed to even); the pejorative sense developed from the idea of an unlucky odd number. Most likely derived from Indo-European *leikʷ- 'to remain, leave behind'.
Meaning:
'Evil', 'bad luck', 'misfortune'; as a personification, the spirit that brings these.
First Use:
Attested in Slavic folktale traditions; widely recorded in 19th-century Russian, Ukrainian, Belarusian, and Polish folklore collections.

Cultural Significance

Likho is treated as a real risk in proverbial speech: one should not 'wake' her, name her too freely, or seek out misfortune. Encountering Likho is understood as an explanation for sudden ruin.
