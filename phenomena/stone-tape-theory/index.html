Stone Tape Theory

anomaly Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

A pseudoscientific hypothesis that strong emotional or traumatic events are 'recorded' onto the surrounding stone, masonry, or other materials of a location, and replayed under suitable conditions as a haunting. Popularised by Nigel Kneale's 1972 BBC television play The Stone Tape; the underlying idea has older roots in 19th- and 20th-century parapsychology.

Details

Origin:
As an interpretive framework rather than a localised phenomenon, the theory is invoked worldwide. Its strongest cultural footprint is in the United Kingdom, where it originated, and in English-language paranormal media generally.
Regions:
United Kingdom, Ireland, United States, Canada, Australia
First Reported:
1972-12-25

Full Account

The 'Stone Tape theory' is a parapsychological hypothesis that buildings, especially old stone buildings, can store imprints of intense emotional or traumatic past events much as a magnetic tape stores audio, and that under suitable conditions sensitive observers can perceive these recordings as visual, auditory, or affective hauntings. The name and modern popular form are owed to Nigel Kneale's BBC play The Stone Tape, broadcast on 25 December 1972, in which a research team investigates a haunted Victorian mansion as an apparent recording medium. Kneale's framing drew on older ideas: Charles Babbage's 1837 speculation that sound permanently impresses itself on the air; Edmund Gurney and Eleanor Sidgwick's 19th-century Society for Psychical Research speculations; H. H. Price's 1940s 'psychic ether' hypothesis; and especially T. C. Lethbridge's 1961 book Ghost and Ghoul, which proposed that emotional energy is stored in the local energy field of natural features. The 'Stone Tape' label is now applied across paranormal media to so-called residual hauntings, in which an apparition repeats the same actions in the same location regardless of observers. Mainstream scientists reject the theory as having no plausible physical mechanism; the geologist and skeptic Sharon Hill has argued that the term itself misleadingly suggests scientific credibility for a speculation lacking any physical basis.

Etymology

Origin:
Coined by Nigel Kneale for his 1972 BBC television play The Stone Tape; the play's hypothesis was rapidly adopted by paranormal writers as the 'Stone Tape theory'.
Meaning:
Analogy between the storage of past events in stone or masonry and the magnetic storage of audio on a recording tape.
First Use:
1972 (Kneale, The Stone Tape, BBC)

Timeline

  • 1837 — Charles Babbage, in The Ninth Bridgewater Treatise, argues that spoken words leave permanent impressions on air molecules, theoretically retrievable (Cited later as a prototype for stone-tape thinking)
  • 1882 — Edmund Gurney and Eleanor Sidgwick of the Society for Psychical Research begin discussing the possibility that environments retain impressions of past events
  • 1940 — H. H. Price, in his SPR presidential address, proposes 'psychic ether' as a medium that could store emotional impressions and replay them to sensitive observers
  • 1961 — T. C. Lethbridge publishes Ghost and Ghoul, arguing that energy fields around natural features store emotional events (Direct intellectual precursor of the Stone Tape hypothesis)
  • 1972-12-25 — BBC2 broadcasts Nigel Kneale's television play The Stone Tape (Names and popularises the modern Stone Tape hypothesis)
