Full Account

The Luferlang (also Lufferlang) is a creature of North American logging-camp folklore, belonging to the genre known as 'fearsome critters'. Its canonical description is from Henry H. Tryon's 1939 Fearsome Critters: a horse-like beast with all four legs triple-jointed, allowing it to run equally fast in any direction, a dark blue stripe down the spine, and a bushy, swivel-jointed tail set in the middle of its back. It is reputed to be exceptionally dangerous and likely to attack without provocation, but bites only once a year — though, the tradition holds, that single bite is invariably fatal. The standard defences cited by Tryon are unusually specific: holding up a large mirror so the doubled image confuses the beast and drives it off, or wearing or displaying an orange-coloured handkerchief, which is said to give 'full protection'. Like other fearsome critters — the Hodag, the Snoligoster, the Hidebehind, the Squonk — the Luferlang originated as an oral tall-tale tradition in the logging camps of the Great Lakes and northeast United States, used to entertain seasoned loggers and to haze new recruits. It survives almost entirely as a literary tradition, principally through Tryon's book.

Cultural Significance

Audiences of these tall tales never literally believed in fearsome critters; the tradition functions as occupational humour and initiation.
