Kappa

Overview

The kappa (河童, 'river child') is one of the most famous yōkai in Japanese folklore: a child-sized amphibious water-spirit with a turtle-like shell, beaked face, and a water-filled dish (sara) on its head whose drying or spilling renders it powerless.

Details

Origin:
Found throughout Japanese folklore, with strong regional concentrations in Kyushu (garappa, hyōsube) and Tōhoku (Tōno).
Regions:
Kyushu, Tōhoku (Iwate, especially Tōno), Kantō, Kansai, Shikoku
First Reported:
1712

Full Account

The kappa (河童, 'river child'; also kawatarō, kawako, garappa, and at least eighty regional names) is a class of water-yokai in Japanese folklore, prototypically the size of a human child, greenish, with a turtle-like carapace, webbed hands and feet, a beaked face, and a circular depression on the crown of the head — the sara — which holds water. The water in the sara is the kappa's source of supernatural power; if it spills or dries out, the kappa is paralysed or killed, which gives rise to the well-known folk defense of bowing deeply (the polite kappa returns the bow, spilling the dish). Kappa are river- and pond-dwelling, associated with sumo wrestling, with attempts to drag horses, cattle, and people into the water, and with the extraction of the mythical 'shirikodama' (尻子玉) — a small ball of life-essence said to lie inside the human anus. They are notoriously fond of cucumbers (giving the name kappamaki to cucumber sushi rolls). Early systematic descriptions appear in the Edo-period encyclopedia Wakan Sansai Zue (和漢三才図会, 1712) by Terajima Ryōan, and the kappa is depicted in Toriyama Sekien's Gazu Hyakki Yagyō (1776). Folkloric documentation continued under modern researchers, especially Yanagita Kunio (1875–1962), whose Tōno Monogatari (遠野物語, 1910) records numerous kappa accounts from Tōno, Iwate, and Lafcadio Hearn (1850–1904), who wrote on kappa in his collections of Japanese folklore. The kappa remains a beloved figure of Japanese popular culture, appearing on signage, in advertising (notably the Kappa beverage and snack brand), in manga, film (Akira Kurosawa's Dreams (1990) and Hayao Miyazaki's Spirited Away (2001) feature kappa-like figures), and as a regional symbol in towns such as Tōno, Ushiku, and Saga.

Cultural Significance

Modern Japanese culture treats the kappa as folklore rather than living belief, while integrating it deeply into regional identity, signage, and commercial branding.
