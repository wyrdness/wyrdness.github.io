Nang Tani

Overview

A female tree spirit of Thai (and Lao and Cambodian) folklore, believed to inhabit clumps of the wild banana Musa acuminata (kluai tani). She is one of the best-known Nang Mai (tree spirits) and is generally benevolent, though said to harm men who have wronged women.

Full Account

Nang Tani (Thai: นางตานี, 'Lady of Tani') is a female tree spirit of Thai, Lao, and Cambodian folklore, also known as Phi Tani ('Ghost of Tani') or Phrai Tani ('Nymph of Tani'). She is one of the most prominent Nang Mai (Thai female tree spirits) and is bound to clumps of the wild banana Musa acuminata, called kluai tani in Thai — a non-cultivated species whose fruit is inedible, growing at village outskirts and field edges and traditionally avoided near homes for that reason. The spirit is described as a beautiful young woman with a greenish complexion that blends with foliage, red lips compared to ripe ivy gourd fruit, abundant black unbound hair, and a green traditional Thai costume; her feet hover slightly above the ground, and modern depictions sometimes show her lower body fading into wisps from the trunk of the banana. Nang Tani is generally benevolent — said to remain hidden within her tree, become visible on full-moon nights, and even offer alms to passing Buddhist monks — but folklore warns she may harm men who have wronged women. Cutting a tree from her clump is believed to bring misfortune. Devotional practice includes tying coloured satin cloth around the tree, leaving sweets, incense, and flowers, and wearing protective amulets bearing her image. The figure has a strong presence in 20th- and 21st-century Thai cinema, beginning with the 1967 classic Nang Phrai Tani.

Etymology

Origin:
Thai นาง (nang, 'lady') + ตานี (tani), the name of the wild banana species (Musa acuminata, kluai tani) she inhabits.
Meaning:
Literally 'Lady of (the) Tani (banana)'.
First Use:
Long-standing Thai folk tradition; well-attested in 20th-century Thai popular culture.

Cultural Significance

Wild banana (kluai tani) is treated with caution: its inedible fruit and ghost-association lead Thai households to avoid planting it near homes.
