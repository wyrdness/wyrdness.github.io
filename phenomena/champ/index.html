Champ

cryptid Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

An aquatic cryptid said to inhabit Lake Champlain on the border of Vermont, New York, and Quebec. Reported as a long-necked, serpentine animal in the loch-monster mould, with several hundred reported sightings since the 19th century.

Details

Origin:
Lake Champlain, a 125-mile-long freshwater lake forming part of the border between Vermont and New York and extending into the Canadian province of Quebec.
Regions:
Vermont, New York, Quebec, Champlain Valley
First Reported:
1819

Full Account

Champ is the popular name for a purported large aquatic cryptid said to inhabit Lake Champlain, a long, deep freshwater lake straddling the border of Vermont, New York, and the Canadian province of Quebec. Reports describe a serpentine or long-necked creature commonly compared with the Loch Ness Monster. Iroquois tradition records giant serpents in the lake — the Mohawk Onyare'kowa — long before European contact, and 19th-century newspaper reports describe sightings such as those of 'Captain Crum' (1819) and Sheriff Nathan Mooney (1883). The most famous modern piece of evidence is the 1977 photograph by Sandra Mansi, taken on July 5, 1977 at a bay on the Vermont side of the lake; the photograph's negative was discarded, and analysts have noted that the bay is only ~14 feet deep, complicating biological interpretations. Both Vermont (1982) and the New York town of Port Henry have passed measures protecting Champ from harm. The creature is one of the most prominent North American lake-monster legends and an important regional cultural and tourism symbol; mainstream scientific consensus regards Champ as a folkloric and misidentification phenomenon rather than a confirmed animal.

Also Known As

  • Champy (Vermont, USA)
  • Lake Champlain Monster (Lake Champlain (USA / Canada))
  • Onyare'kowa (Mohawk, Iroquois territory; 'great horned serpent' in oral tradition)

Etymology

Origin:
Anglophone shortening of 'Champlain', the lake's name, which itself commemorates French explorer Samuel de Champlain (1567–1635).
Meaning:
Affectionate diminutive of 'Champlain'.
First Use:
Popularised in late-19th-century regional press; widespread by the 20th century.

Timeline

  • 1819-01-01 — Newspaper account of 'Capt. Crum' on Lake Champlain reports a creature estimated at ~187 feet long (Earliest English-language documented sighting)
  • 1873-08-01 — P. T. Barnum offers a reward for Champ's hide (Triggers a major wave of late-19th-century newspaper reports)
  • 1883-07-01 — Sheriff Nathan H. Mooney reports a serpent-like creature 25–30 feet long in Lake Champlain near Plattsburgh (Most-cited 19th-century law-enforcement witness report)
  • 1977-07-05 — Sandra Mansi photographs an apparent long-necked creature in Lake Champlain north of St. Albans, Vermont (Most famous Champ photograph; long called the best lake-monster photograph ever taken)
  • 1982-03-01 — Vermont House of Representatives passes a resolution protecting Champ from harm (Formal civic protection of the cryptid)
  • 2003-06-01 — Fauna Communications survey claims to record echolocation-like clicks in Lake Champlain (Acoustic-evidence claim; not peer-reviewed and contested)
  • 2005-05-31 — Bob Aurora and Pete Bodette video apparent large creature in Lake Champlain off Burlington (Most-discussed video evidence of the 21st century)

Cultural Significance

Local tradition treats Champ as a real, if elusive, animal of the lake; protection ordinances reflect both genuine belief and tourism-friendly civic identity.
