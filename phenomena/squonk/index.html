Squonk

cryptid Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

A 'fearsome critter' of North American lumberjack folklore, said to inhabit the hemlock forests of northern Pennsylvania. The squonk is famously ugly, weeps continuously, and is said to dissolve into its own tears when cornered.

Details

Origin:
Confined in lumberjack folklore to the hemlock forests of northern Pennsylvania, USA. Cox notes a possible secondary range in adjacent forested mountain country.
Regions:
Pennsylvania, Allegheny Plateau
First Reported:
1910

Full Account

The squonk is a creature of the lumberjack 'fearsome critters' tradition of the late 19th and early 20th century United States. First described in print in William T. Cox's 1910 Fearsome Creatures of the Lumberwoods, with a Few Desert and Mountain Beasts, the squonk is said to inhabit the hemlock forests of northern Pennsylvania. Its skin is described as ill-fitting, covered in warts and moles, so that the creature is utterly miserable about its appearance and weeps perpetually. The squonk is therefore most easily tracked at night by following its tear-stained trail. The most famous tale, given by Cox and attributed to a hunter named J. P. Wentling of Pennsylvania, recounts how Wentling supposedly captured a squonk in a sack only to discover, upon arrival home, that the creature had dissolved into a puddle of tears and bubbles. The squonk has no zoological reality but has retained an outsized place in American folklore and popular culture, including a song by Genesis (1976), references in Steely Dan, and the annual Squonkapalooza festival in Johnstown, Pennsylvania.

Also Known As

  • Lacrimacorpus dissolvens (Latin (mock-scientific))

Etymology

Origin:
Lumberjack tall-tale tradition of the northeastern United States; first attested in print in Cox's 1910 monograph.
Meaning:
Origin obscure; likely an imitative coinage from logging-camp slang. Cox's mock-Latin Lacrimacorpus dissolvens means 'tear-body that dissolves'.
First Use:
1910 (Cox, Fearsome Creatures of the Lumberwoods)

Timeline

  • 1910 — William T. Cox publishes Fearsome Creatures of the Lumberwoods, with a Few Desert and Mountain Beasts (Judd & Detweiler), describing the squonk and naming it Lacrimacorpus dissolvens (First print appearance of the squonk)
  • 1939 — Henry H. Tryon publishes Fearsome Critters, an expanded compendium that retains and elaborates the squonk entry
  • 1976 — Genesis releases 'Squonk' on the album A Trick of the Tail (Brings the squonk into late-20th-century popular culture)
