Ibaraki-dōji

entity_spirit Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

Ibaraki-dōji is a celebrated oni (demon) of Heian-period Japanese legend, the chief retainer of the demon king Shuten-dōji and the antagonist of the famous Rashōmon / Modori-bashi episode in which the warrior Watanabe no Tsuna severs its arm.

Details

Origin:
The Ibaraki-dōji legend is centered on Heian-period Kyoto and on the surrounding regions of Settsu (modern Osaka, Hyōgo) and Echigo (modern Niigata).
Regions:
Kyoto, Settsu, Echigo / Niigata, Mount Ōe, Tanba, Honshu
First Reported:
Heian/Kamakura period

Full Account

Ibaraki-dōji (茨木童子) is one of the most famous oni in Japanese folklore, traditionally the chief lieutenant of the great oni-king Shuten-dōji of Mount Ōe. Two main birth-origin traditions place the demon either at Sunagodzuka in Echigo (modern Niigata) or in Settsu (modern Osaka / Hyōgo). The character's gender is ambiguous in the source tradition, sometimes a male oni and sometimes a kijo (female oni). The most famous narrative is set in late Heian Kyoto: Watanabe no Tsuna, one of the Four Guardian Kings (Shitennō) of Minamoto no Yorimitsu (Raikō), encounters a beautiful young woman who reveals herself as Ibaraki-dōji and attempts to abduct him, either at the Modori-bashi bridge over the Ichijō or at the Rashōmon gate. Tsuna severs the demon's arm with his sword Higekiri (also called Onikiri / Onikirimaru). Warned by an onmyōji (often Abe no Seimei) to confine himself for seven days, Tsuna is on the seventh night tricked by Ibaraki-dōji, who appears disguised as his aunt and reclaims the arm. The episode is a foundational story-cycle of the demon-slaying repertoire and is told across the Konjaku monogatari, the Tsurugi-no-maki of the Heike monogatari, the Genpei jōsuiki, the Taiheiki, the Settsuyō gundan (1701), and a long line of nō, kabuki and ukiyo-e treatments.

Etymology

Origin:
Ibaraki-dōji combines the place-name Ibaraki — variously read as Ibaraki in Settsu (modern Osaka) or as the Ibaraki of Echigo — with dōji (童子, 'child'), a term used for oni offspring and for young oni-attendants rather than for biological children.
Meaning:
'Oni-child of Ibaraki'.
First Use:
Medieval otogi-zōshi and gunki-monogatari (Heike monogatari Tsurugi-no-maki, Genpei jōsuiki, Taiheiki) of the 13th–14th centuries; the Settsuyō gundan (1701) consolidates the Settsu tradition.

Timeline

  • Pre-contact — Compilation of Konjaku monogatari (early 12th century) and Heike monogatari Tsurugi-no-maki (13th century), containing early forms of the Watanabe no Tsuna / Ibaraki-dōji episode (Earliest attestations of the legend in Japanese literature)
  • Pre-contact — Composition of the nō play Rashōmon (attributed to Kanze school, 15th century) (Early dramatic treatment of the Rashōmon variant)
  • 1701-01-01 — Settsuyō gundan published, consolidating the Settsu version of Ibaraki-dōji's birth narrative (Major Edo-period source for the Settsu tradition)
  • 1883-01-01 — Kabuki play Ibaraki (茨木) premieres, written for Onoe Kikugorō V (Foundational kabuki dramatization of the post-encounter retrieval scene; remains a canonical role)
  • 1889-01-01 — Tsukioka Yoshitoshi includes 'Old Woman Taking Away the Oni's Arm' (Rōba ki wo motte saru no zu) in Shinkei Sanjūrokkaisen, completed 1892 (Most-reproduced visual treatment of the Ibaraki-dōji legend in modern times)

Cultural Significance

Severed oni arms must be guarded with ritual seclusion (the seven-day kessai) overseen by an onmyōji; oni can shapeshift into human form, especially as elderly female relatives, and so social trust itself becomes a site of demonic ambush.

Related Phenomena

  • Shuten-dōji — The oni-king of Mount Ōe whom Ibaraki-dōji serves as chief lieutenant
  • Oni — The general class of Japanese demonic ogres to which Ibaraki-dōji belongs
  • Kijo — Female oni; some traditions class Ibaraki-dōji as kijo
