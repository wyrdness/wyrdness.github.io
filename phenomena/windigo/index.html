Windigo

Overview

A spirit-being of Algonquian-language traditions — Ojibwe wiindigoo, Cree wīhtikōw — embodying the moral horror of cannibalism, of insatiable greed, and of the heart turned to ice. Both a being met in story and a name for a culturally specific psychological transformation.

Details

Origin:
The Windigo is a being of the boreal forest of northern North America, associated specifically with the territories of Algonquian-speaking peoples — Ojibwe / Anishinaabe, Cree, Innu, Naskapi, Atikamekw, Saulteaux — across present-day Canada and the northern United States.

Full Account

The Windigo (Ojibwe wiindigoo; Cree wīhtikōw, often anglicised 'Wendigo') is a powerful spiritual being in the traditions of many Algonquian-speaking peoples of the boreal forest of North America, including the Anishinaabe (Ojibwe), Cree, Innu, Naskapi, and Atikamekw. The reconstructed Proto-Algonquian form, *wi·nteko·wa, may have meant 'owl'. The Windigo is associated with winter, famine, ice, isolation, and the moral catastrophe of human cannibalism. It is described in much of the literature as gaunt and monstrous, with a heart of ice; in Ojibwe tradition specifically, the figure is often understood not as a simple monster but as a being into which a human can transform — through cannibalism, through unchecked greed, or through the imbalance of mind that may follow famine. The English form 'Wendigo' was popularised by Algernon Blackwood's 1910 novella The Wendigo. From the early 20th century, anthropologists including Morton I. Teicher described a culture-bound 'Windigo psychosis' (1961) characterised by a craving for human flesh; this clinical category was sharply criticised by later scholars (notably Lou Marano in the 1980s) as a colonial misreading of Indigenous moral and spiritual concepts. Historical cases entangled with the figure include the 1879 trial and execution of the Plains Cree man Swift Runner (who killed and consumed his family near Fort Saskatchewan during the winter of 1878), and the 1907 arrest of the Oji-Cree leader Jack Fiddler and his brother Joseph for windigo-related killings in Sandy Lake. Indigenous scholars including Basil H. Johnston (Anishinaabe) and James B. Waldram have re-centred the Windigo as a sophisticated moral and spiritual concept rather than a horror trope.

Etymology

Origin:
From Algonquian language family — Ojibwe wiindigoo, Cree wīhtikōw — with reconstructed Proto-Algonquian *wi·nteko·wa.
Meaning:
The Proto-Algonquian root is sometimes glossed as 'owl'; the term in living Algonquian languages denotes a specific class of cannibal spirit. English 'Wendigo' was anglicised from Algonquian forms and popularised by Algernon Blackwood in 1910.
First Use:
Algonquian usage long predates contact; English-language form 'Wendigo' popularised in 1910 by Algernon Blackwood's novella of the same name.

Cultural Significance

In Algonquian teachings the Windigo is a spiritual reality and a moral warning. Cannibalism, greed, and the abandonment of communal obligation are seen not as ordinary crimes but as the doorway to a kind of spiritual transformation.
