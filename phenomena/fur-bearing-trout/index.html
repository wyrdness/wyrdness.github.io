Fur-Bearing Trout

cryptid Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

The fur-bearing trout is a legendary fish, North American and Icelandic in origin, said to grow a thick coat of fur to survive cold northern waters; in practice it is a long-running tall tale and taxidermy hoax in which a real trout is mounted with rabbit fur attached.

Details

Origin:
The fur-bearing trout is a folkloric and tourist tradition of North America (especially the northern Rockies, the Great Lakes region, and Canada) and a folktale tradition of Iceland. There is no real biological range.
Regions:
Colorado (Arkansas River folklore), Montana, Lake Superior region, Ontario, Canada, Alberta, Canada, Iceland
First Reported:
1854-01-01

Full Account

The fur-bearing trout is a creature of North American and Icelandic folk tradition, supposed to be a species of trout that has grown a thick coat of white or pale fur to survive in the frigid lakes and rivers of the far north. The most popular American origin story claims that two (or four) jugs of hair tonic were spilled into the Arkansas River near Salida, Colorado, with the alleged result that the local trout grew fur. In practice, the 'fur-bearing trout' is a tall-tale-and-taxidermy tradition: real trout are stuffed and mounted with sections of rabbit pelt or other fur attached, and exhibited in lodges, taverns, and small museums as humorous curiosities, alongside such other tall-tale taxidermies as the jackalope. The Icelandic Loðsilungur, attested in 19th-century Icelandic folktale collections (including by Jón Árnason, 1862–64), is more sinister — a creature said to be poisonous and inedible, created by demons or giants, with a recorded carcass-find at Svína-vatn (Pig Lake) in 1854 illustrated in the 1855 newspaper Norðri. The earliest American printing of the legend is generally cited as a 1929 article by J. H. Hicken in Montana Wildlife magazine. A famous mounted hoax specimen by the taxidermist Ross C. Jobe of Sault Ste. Marie, Ontario, is held by National Museums Scotland (the Royal Scottish Museum), and another resides in the Royal Tyrrell Museum and the Banff Park Museum, Canada. Two natural phenomena have been suggested as superficially supportive: Saprolegnia ('cotton mold'), a parasitic water-mould that produces fur-like growths on infected fish, and Mirapinna esau, a real but unrelated whalefish bearing hair-like skin extensions described by Bertelsen and Marshall in 1956 from specimens taken in the Azores.

Etymology

Origin:
An English-language tall-tale name, descriptive of the supposed creature; first appearing in print in J. H. Hicken's 1929 article 'My Pet Trout' in Montana Wildlife magazine.
Meaning:
A trout whose body is covered in fur for cold-weather insulation.
First Use:
1929 (Hicken, 'My Pet Trout', Montana Wildlife); the Icelandic Loðsilungur attested by Jón Árnason (1862–64).

Cultural Significance

The fur-bearing trout has never been the subject of literal belief in the way Loðsilungur was once viewed in Iceland; it has functioned for over a century as a genre of fishing humour and tourist curiosity.
