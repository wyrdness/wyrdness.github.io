Loup-Garou

shapeshifter Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

The Loup-Garou is the French-language werewolf, transplanted by French settlers into Louisiana (where it became the Rougarou of the bayous), into Haitian and Caribbean Creole cultures (Lougarou, Loogaroo), and into French Canadian / Métis tradition. It is typically a human cursed to transform into a wolf-like or hybrid form by breaking Catholic Lenten observance.

Details

Origin:
The loup-garou tradition is found wherever French and French-Creole communities settled: continental France (especially rural Catholic regions), French Canada, Louisiana (Acadiana and Greater New Orleans), Haiti, and the wider Francophone Caribbean. Métis and Ojibwe communities of the Great Lakes have a related rugaru figure.
Regions:
Continental France, Quebec, Acadia / Maritimes, Louisiana (Acadiana, Greater New Orleans), Haiti, Caribbean Creole world, Great Lakes (Métis, Ojibwe)
First Reported:
12th century

Full Account

The Loup-Garou is the French werewolf — loup ('wolf') plus garou (from Frankish *werwolf, cognate with English werewolf). The term and its variants travelled through French colonisation: in Louisiana French it became Rougarou (also spelled roux-ga-roux, rugaroo), in Haitian Creole Lougarou, and in the wider Caribbean Loogaroo. Continental French Catholic folklore typically frames the loup-garou as a human punished for breaking religious observance — most often by failing to keep Lent for seven consecutive years — who transforms into a wolf-like creature. In Louisiana the Rougarou is most often described as a creature with a human body and the head of a wolf or dog that prowls the swamps, sugar-cane fields and bayous of Acadiana and Greater New Orleans, and is invoked to enforce Catholic discipline (especially Lent) and to frighten children. A common motif is the curse-transfer: the rougarou must transmit its curse by drawing the blood of a victim within a fixed period (commonly cited as 101 days), or remain trapped in beast form. In Haitian Vodou-influenced tradition the Lougarou is more often associated with witchcraft and pacts. Métis and Ojibwe communities in the Great Lakes have a related rugaru, which the writer Peter Matthiessen suggests was carried by French Canadian trappers into Indigenous oral tradition. The phenomenon is treated by folklorists as a transatlantic Catholic-Creole-Indigenous synthesis rather than a literal cryptid.

Etymology

Origin:
From French loup-garou: loup ('wolf') + garou (Old French garoul, from Frankish *werwulf 'man-wolf'), formally a redundant compound 'wolf-werewolf'. The Louisiana form rougarou shows characteristic L↔R liquid metathesis common in Louisiana French oral tradition.
Meaning:
Werewolf; a human transformed into a wolf or wolf-like creature.
First Use:
Old French garoul attested in 12th-century lays (Marie de France's Bisclavret, c. 1170s); loup-garou as a compound in standard French by the late Middle Ages; rougarou in Louisiana French by the 19th century.

Cultural Significance

In Catholic French and Louisiana tradition, breaking Lent (especially seven years in a row) was widely believed to risk transformation into a loup-garou; counting tricks (e.g. leaving thirteen grains of rice at the door) and ritual blood-drawing were cited as defences.
