Loch Ness Monster

cryptid Danger: unknown Evidence: unknown

Overview

A large aquatic cryptid said to inhabit Loch Ness, a deep freshwater lake in the Scottish Highlands. The most reported lake monster in the world, with a continuous record of sightings since the 1930s and a distant precursor in the 6th-century Life of St. Columba.

Details

Origin:
Reports are confined to Loch Ness in the Scottish Highlands, with a minor secondary tradition (the Morag) in the nearby Loch Morar.
Regions:
Scottish Highlands, Great Glen, Inverness-shire
First Reported:
565 CE

Full Account

The Loch Ness Monster, popularly known as Nessie, is a large aquatic cryptid reported in Loch Ness, a deep freshwater loch on the Great Glen Fault in the Scottish Highlands. The modern phenomenon began in 1933 after a new road, the A82, was built along the loch's northern shore and opened up clear views of the water. Witnesses typically describe a long-necked, dark-coloured animal with one or more humps, sometimes interpreted as a relict plesiosaur. A succession of investigations — culminating in the Loch Ness Phenomena Investigation Bureau (1962–1972), Robert Rines's Academy of Applied Science underwater work in the 1970s, the 1987 sonar sweep Operation Deepscan, and the 2018–2019 environmental DNA survey led by Neil Gemmell of the University of Otago — has consistently failed to recover any large unknown vertebrate. The eDNA survey detected no reptile DNA but a notably strong eel signal. The scientific consensus treats Nessie as a folkloric phenomenon explained by misidentification of waves, boat wakes, otters, deer, large eels, and other mundane phenomena, alongside a long history of confirmed hoaxes. The legend supports a substantial Highland tourism economy and remains internationally recognisable.

Also Known As

  • Nessie (English, Scotland)
  • Niseag (Scottish Gaelic, Scotland)

Etymology

Origin:
The English phrase 'Loch Ness Monster' was coined in May 1933 by Inverness Courier editor Evan Barron, in instructions to reporter Alex Campbell to use that wording in coverage of the Mackay sighting. The diminutive 'Nessie' followed in popular usage.
Meaning:
Loch Ness, the lake; 'monster' in the popular cryptid sense.
First Use:
1933 (Inverness Courier, Scotland)

Timeline

  • c. 565 — Adomnán records Columba's encounter with a 'water beast' in the River Ness (Earliest written reference cited in modern Nessie literature, although it concerns the river rather than the loch)
  • 1933-04-15 — John Mackay and Aldie Mackay report a large 'whale-like' creature in the loch (First widely-reported modern sighting; published 2 May 1933 in the Inverness Courier under Alex Campbell's byline)
  • 1933-07-22 — George Spicer and his wife report a long-necked creature crossing the road in front of their car near Whitefield (First widely-reported land sighting; established the long-necked image of Nessie)
  • 1933-11-12 — Hugh Gray takes the first published photograph of a putative monster near Foyers (First photograph; widely argued to show a dog with a stick or a swan)
  • 1934-04-19 — Robert Kenneth Wilson takes the 'Surgeon's Photograph' (Became the iconic image of Nessie for 60 years)
  • 1960-04-23 — Tim Dinsdale films a moving dark hump at Foyers (First widely-circulated film footage; later analysed by the Royal Air Force JARIC)
  • 1962 — Loch Ness Phenomena Investigation Bureau (LNIB) founded by David James, Peter Scott, Richard Fitter, and others (First sustained scientific watching programme)
  • 1972-08-08 — Robert Rines's Academy of Applied Science team obtains underwater 'flipper' photographs in Urquhart Bay (Used by Rines and Peter Scott to propose the binomial Nessiteras rhombopteryx)
  • 1987-10 — Operation Deepscan deploys 24 boats with echo-sounders across the loch in a synchronized sweep, led by Adrian Shine (Largest sonar survey ever attempted at Loch Ness; reports a small number of unexplained mid-water contacts)
  • 1994-03 — Christian Spurling tells David Martin and Alastair Boyd that the 1934 Surgeon's Photograph was a hoax constructed with a toy submarine (Definitive debunking of the iconic image)
  • 2003 — BBC team led by Ian Florence and Hugh Davies sweeps the entire loch with 600 sonar beams and satellite tracking, finding no large animal (Most comprehensive pre-eDNA negative result)
  • 2019-09-05 — Neil Gemmell of the University of Otago releases the results of a 2018–2019 environmental DNA survey of Loch Ness (Found no reptile DNA, no large fish DNA, but high quantities of European eel DNA)

Cultural Significance

Highland tradition long includes water-horse and water-bull beings inhabiting deep lochs; the modern Nessie sits within and partly displaces this older tradition.
