Full Account

The Nachtkrapp ('night raven') is a folkloric night-bird of Germanic tradition, found chiefly in southern Germany and Austria with cognate figures across Central and Northern Europe. In its most widespread form it is described as a large, raven-like nocturnal bird that emerges after dark to seize disobedient or unsupervised children. Some accounts have it carrying children off in a sack or to a hidden nest where they are killed; in milder local variants the so-called Wütender Nachtkrapp ('angry Nachtkrapp') merely terrifies children with its cries and beating wings, while a Guter Nachtkrapp ('good Nachtkrapp') sings them gently to sleep. A northern German variant has wings of iron, giving rise to the byname eiserner Vogel ('iron bird'). The closely related Swedish nattramn is described not as a living bird but as the revenant of an unbaptized child or of someone buried in unconsecrated ground, often depicted in skeletal form. Although clearly a creature of legend rather than zoology, the Nachtkrapp is well-attested in 19th-century German lexicography (notably the Grimms' Deutsches Wörterbuch) and in regional folklore collections, and the figure passed into early-19th-century literature through works such as Johann August Apel's 1810 tale Der Freischütz.

Etymology

Origin:
Compound of Middle High German naht ('night') and krapp/rabe ('raven'). Originally used in older German to denote real nocturnal birds (nightjars, owls, herons) before crystallizing as a folkloric creature.
Meaning:
Literally 'night raven'.
First Use:
Attested in German folkloric and lexicographic sources from at least the 19th century; literary use by Johann August Apel (Der Freischütz, 1810)

Timeline

Cultural Significance

The Nachtkrapp functions as a household disciplinary figure invoked by parents to keep children indoors and in bed after dark.
